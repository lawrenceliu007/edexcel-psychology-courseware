Background: the question behind the study

Stanley Milgram was a social psychologist at University. He began the study in , sixteen years after the end of the Second World War.his workplace / the year

At the Nuremberg trials, Nazi officers accused of mass murder used the same defence: “”. The world's answer was that these men must have been unusually cruel or mentally ill.four words you will meet again in 1.1.1

Milgram was testing a different idea: that ordinary, mentally healthy people would obey, and that the cause lay in the rather than in their .two nouns: 情境 / 人格

Two words students lose marks on
TermDefinition 定义（背原文）Why it matters here
obedience 服从Following a from someone perceived to have .In Milgram there is one man giving one order.
conformity 从众Changing your behaviour to match a or — no order is given.Covered in 1.1.4–1.1.7. Not this chapter.
中文理解：为什么这个区分决定整章？
如果服从来自人格，那只有“某种人”才会做坏事，Milgram 的实验就没什么好讲的。
如果服从来自情境，那么任何人都可能——只要情境对。Milgram 的三个变式实验就是为了证明第二种：他不停地改情境，看服从会不会跟着变。
所以考试里问 “describe” 你要写程序，问 “evaluate” 你要落到情境 vs 人格这个争论。

Moral strain 道德压力

— the distress a person feels when what they are told to do clashes with what they believe is right.the English term

Signs Milgram observed 观察到的表现（写中文即可）

· 、、（三个生理反应）
· biting their lips until they 　· digging their fingernails into their own flesh
· nervous fits　· participants had full-blown, uncontrollable seizures

Why this detail wins marks
If participants had calmly pressed to the end, the conclusion would be “some people are born cruel”. Instead they sweated, trembled and bit their lips — they did not want to continue but could not stop. That is what moral strain names, and it is the reason Milgram argued the behaviour came from the situation, not from sadism.

Demand characteristics 需求特征

and (1968) argued participants did not really believe the shocks were real — so their behaviour was not obedience at all, it was going along with what they thought the researcher wanted. That is : cues in a study that tell people what is expected.two surnames / year / the English term

Evidence they DID believe
  • Unmistakable distress — trembling, sweating, three .
  • In post-experiment interviews, said they believed the shocks were real.
  • % were glad to have taken part; only about % regretted it.
  • A follow-up later found no lasting psychological harm.
Evidence some did NOT
  • (2013) re-analysed Milgram's archived tape recordings.
  • A number of participants openly whether the shocks were real during the session.

§13 · Experiment 13 · the ordinary man gives ordersANSWERS → p.18
What changed

The experimenter was called away on a pretended . An “ordinary man” — a pretending to be another participant — then took over and suggested raising the shock by V after each wrong answer. There were participants.

No uniform, no lab coat, no position.what he lacked

Result

Only % — of the 20 — obeyed fully. This is the of the three variations.

Typical response

Many used the possible shocks, with him, or went through the motions without really following his suggestions.

What it shows

Obedience depends on — who is seen as entitled to give orders. The same words from someone with no authority have very little power.

中文理解：最贴近“我不是听你的，我是听那个位置的”
“普通人”说的话完全一样，但他没有制服、没有头衔、不代表任何机构。结果：几乎没人听他的。
这说明服从的对象是那个被认可的权威身份，而不是任何一张嘴。

p.16 — Q1 model (2 marks)

① The participant is deceived into believing the study is about memory and learning, and is ordered to give electric shocks that increase with each mistake. ② The researcher responds to hesitation with the standard prod “the experiment requires that you continue”, and the setting includes a laboratory at a prestigious institution — all three are features of Milgram's baseline study.

p.16 — Q2 model (4 marks)

Milgram recruited 40 male participants through a newspaper advert, telling them the study was about memory and learning. At a Yale laboratory each participant was given the role of teacher through a rigged draw, while a confederate played the learner. The teacher read word pairs and gave the learner an electric shock for every wrong answer, increasing it by 15 V each time from 15 V up to 450 V. If the teacher hesitated the experimenter used one of four standard prods, such as “the experiment requires that you continue”. 100% reached 300 V and 65% continued to 450 V.

p.16 — Q3 model (2 marks)

Moral strain is the distress a person experiences when following an order conflicts with their own judgement about what is right. In Milgram's study participants showed it through sweating, trembling, stuttering, nervous laughter and biting their lips until they bled, and three participants had full-blown seizures — showing they continued while deeply uncomfortable rather than obeying calmly.

p.16 — Q4 model (2 marks)

One situational factor is the physical presence of the authority figure. In Experiment 7 the experimenter gave his orders by telephone instead of standing in the room, and obedience fell from 65% to 20.5%. Because nothing else changed, the drop shows that removing the authority's presence makes dissent far more likely — people are much readier to refuse someone they do not have to face.
Alternative: legitimacy — Experiment 13, ordinary man gave the orders, obedience fell to 20% (4 of 20). Or: prestige — Experiment 10, rundown office block in Bridgeport, 47.5% still obeyed, so prestige contributes but is much weaker than presence.

p.16 — Q5 model (3 marks)

① Milgram described the procedure to 40 psychiatrists, who predicted that only about 1.2% — roughly one person in a thousand — would go to 450 V. ② In fact 65% (26 of 40) did. ③ The huge gap matters because even experts who study disturbed behaviour assumed obedience requires an unusual personality; the result supports Milgram's situational explanation and shows how poorly people predict the power of the situation — which is exactly why the finding is important.

p.17 — Q6 model (8 marks)

¶1 Method. A strength is that the procedure was highly standardised: every participant heard the same protests at the same voltages and faced the same four prods, so the study is replicable and the variations are genuinely comparable. However, this control was bought at the cost of ecological validity — pressing switches in a laboratory is not the same as carrying out orders inside a real institution.

¶2 Ethics. Participants were deceived about the aim, the draw and the shocks, could not give informed consent, and were pressured not to withdraw; several showed extreme distress and three had seizures. This matters beyond welfare: the deception means we cannot be sure participants were showing real obedience rather than responding to demand characteristics, as Orne and Holland (1968) argued. Milgram replied that participants were distressed precisely because they believed it — and 84% later said they were glad to have taken part.

¶3 Sample and validity. 40 male volunteers from one American city, recruited by advert and paid $4.50 — androcentric and culturally narrow, so the 65% figure cannot be generalised to women. Blass (1999) partly answers this, finding broadly similar rates in replications outside the USA.

¶4 Conclusion. The conclusion is contested: Haslam, Reicher and Birney (2014) argue participants obeyed because they identified with the scientific goals rather than because they abandoned their conscience. Yet Burger (2009) found 70% still continued at 150 V decades later, and all three variations show obedience shifting with the situation alone. On balance the situational explanation is well supported for the laboratory task, but should not be stretched to explain real-world atrocities on its own.
