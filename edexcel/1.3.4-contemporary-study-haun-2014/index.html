1.3.4 CONTEMPORARY STUDY · HAUN ET AL. (2014)
Children conform to the behaviour of peers; other great apes stick with what they know

Your option study for Topic A: two-year-old children, chimpanzees and orangutans face the same choice, and only one species copies the crowd.
Topic A · Social psychology Studies: contemporary (option) Spec 1.3.4

What this study is really asking

  1. Is conformity uniquely human? Haun et al. put children, chimpanzees and orangutans through the same task: learn one way to get a reward, then watch three peers use a different way.
  2. Children switched. 12 of 18 moved to the majority's strategy; they conformed in over half of trials.
  3. Apes stayed. 10 of 12 chimpanzees and 8 of 12 orangutans kept their own learned strategy.
  4. Conclusion: in the same situation, it was the children who adjusted their behaviour to the peer majority; conforming to peers runs deep in human development.

1. Getting Started

You have learned one reliable way to get something you want. Then three people you know, one after another, all use a different way, and it works just as well for them. Now it is your turn with three goes. Do you stick with what you learned, or switch to match the group?

That is the whole experiment, and it was run on two very different kinds of participant: two-year-old children, and great apes.

🎯 Have a guess first No notes

Both strategies get the reward equally. Who is most likely to copy the majority?

The children, by a wide margin. And that gap is the finding.

中文理解：这个实验的妙处
三个物种面对一模一样的题目：自己学会一个拿奖励的办法，再看三个同伴用另一个办法，最后给自己三次机会。
因为任务相同，谁跟风、谁固执就直接可比。孩子大批换方法，黑猩猩和红毛猩猩几乎都守着自己的老办法。从众这件事，在人类身上扎得格外深，两岁就已经成形。

2. Why Compare Species?

Haun et al. investigated conformity between human and non-human peers to see whether social conformity was specific to humans.

Primates were used because they are the most similar animal to humans. If even our closest relatives ignore the peer majority, then copying the crowd is a deeply human habit; if they copy too, conformity is something much older than our species.

conspecific: a member of one's own species.

Each participant watched three familiar conspecific peers: children watched other children, chimpanzees watched other chimpanzees, orangutans watched other orangutans. The comparison stays fair because everyone is tempted by members of their own kind.

3. Aim

Aim： to investigate human and non-human animals' tendencies to adjust their behaviour to a majority of their peers in a similar situation. Specifically, to investigate whether two-year-old children and primates (chimpanzees and orangutans) would adjust an individually acquired behaviour strategy, following exposure to a majority of peers displaying an equally effective but different behaviour strategy.

Two phrases in the aim do a lot of work:

  • "Individually acquired": the strategy was learned alone, before any peer pressure appeared.
  • "Equally effective": the majority's way works just as well, so switching is pure social influence; there is no practical reason to move.

5. The Box

The apparatus was a box with three sections of different colours, arranged horizontally. The two outer sections were of equal height; the third section, in the middle, was shorter. Each section top had a hole in it.

  • Placing a ball into a hole would trigger a reward dispensed at the bottom of that section.
  • The researchers controlled which holes had an active trigger to release the reward.
  • The box was placed on the ground for the child participants; for the primates it was attached to the outside of a steel mesh of an observation room.
  • Rewards: peanuts for the primates, chocolate drops for the children.
Figure 1 — Three coloured sections, one active trigger, a reward waiting at the bottom of the correct one.
中文理解：为什么要设计三个格子
一个格子玩不出花样：要么拿奖励要么拿不到，没有"跟风"的空间。
三个格子里只有一个是活的。参与者先学会"我的格子"（比如蓝色），再看同伴都去按另一个格子（比如绿色）。第三种颜色留给编码员当"other response"归类用。学会的、被示范的、第三个，三种选择各有去处，数据才干净。

6. Phase One: Learn

During the initial phase, all participants learned that a ball would go into any hole, but that only one would dispense a reward.

  • Participants were allowed to drop a ball into any section until they learned that only one colour section dispensed a reward.
  • Learning criterion: dropping a ball into the correct section eight out of ten times.

Why the criterion matters

Eight out of ten shows the strategy was genuinely learned, not guessed. Everything that happens later, switching or staying, is measured against a strategy the participant had already mastered on their own. That is what "individually acquired" means in practice.

7. Phase Two: Watch

The participant was taken two metres away from the box, where they could watch three familiar conspecific peers interact with the box one at a time, seeing them place the ball in a hole and the section dispense a reward.

FeatureDetail
The demonstratorsThe conspecific peers were labelled as demonstrators. They were trained to prefer one colour section of the box, different from the section the participant had learned dispensed the reward.
The showThe first demonstrator placed the ball in the section hole and received a reward, and did this twice. The procedure was repeated for the remaining two demonstrators, producing a majority (six demonstrations in total).
The attention checkThe ball was handed to a demonstrator only when the participant was looking, and the trial was repeated if the participant was not paying attention while the ball was dropped or the reward dispensed.
After the showThe demonstrators were led away, but were still able to see the participant interacting with the box.
中文理解：两个容易被忽略的细节
示范者必须是熟人（familiar），所以测的是对熟悉同伴的从众，对陌生人不算数。
球只在参与者看着的时候发出去，没看就重演一遍。六次示范一次不漏地进入眼睛，后面"没看见所以没换"的解释就被堵死了。

8. Phase Three: Choose

After 30 seconds, the participants were given three balls, one at a time, and allowed to place each ball in a section hole of their choice. All three choices were rewarded regardless of the hole they chose.

The choice of hole was video recorded and coded by two independent observers as one of three responses:

Switch

Change to the majority response, the strategy the demonstrators used.

Stay

Keep their own preference, the strategy they had previously learned.

Other response

Choose the third option, which was neither their preferred nor the demonstrated response.

The reward detail is deliberate

Every hole pays now. The practical value of staying has vanished: switching costs nothing and staying earns nothing extra. Whatever the participant does next is pure social influence, which is exactly what the study wants to isolate.

9. The Score

The number of 'stay' responses was subtracted from the number of 'switch' responses to calculate whether the participants conformed to their majority peer.

Score
What it means
−3
Chose their preferred response every time: total loyalty to what they had learned, no conformity.
+3
Changed their response to match the peer majority every time: total conformity.
Negative
Demonstrates no conformity.
Positive
Demonstrates conformity.
中文理解：一个减法把方向定住
switch 减 stay：换的次数多，得正数，说明跟着大多数人走；守的次数多，得负数，说明守着自己学会的办法。
三个球，两种主要选择的差值落在 −3 到 +3 之间。负数、正数，方向一目了然，不需要复杂的统计。

Figure 2 — Table 6.3 as a picture. Children dominate the "switch" column; the apes dominate the "stay" column.
  • Children, but not primates, were more likely to change from an initial preferred strategy to that of their peers.
  • Further analysis showed children conformed to their peer group in over half of trials.
  • The primate participants almost exclusively stayed with their preferred choice and did not conform to the peer majority.

11. Conclusion

Conclusion： in a situation where children, chimpanzees and orangutans observe a conspecific peer majority perform a behavioural strategy different from one they have learned, it was children who were more likely to adjust their behavioural strategy than primates.

Put back into the language of the topic: conformity to peers shows itself early in human development, by age two, and it shows itself where our closest relatives show none. The human tendency to move with the majority does not need years of schooling or culture to appear.

12. Evaluation · Weaknesses

Artificial task

Watching a conspecific place a ball in a hole and receive a reward is not a typical everyday task. The levels of behaviour change were demonstrated under contrived conditions, which may not reflect how we conform to peers in more everyday situations, such as in the playground.

Captive primates

The study used captive primates, so their behaviour may not usefully reflect that of wild chimpanzees and orangutans.

Individual variation

There is considerable variation in conformity between individuals, which may have influenced the findings. The results may not generalise beyond the sample tested.

No follow-up

We cannot be certain that the influence of the peers had any lasting effect on behaviour change, as this was not tested.

A fairness point worth half a sentence

The same artificial task cuts both ways: conformity to peers was at least objectively measured as 'switch', 'stay' or 'other response'. Contrived does not mean sloppy.

13. Evaluation · Strengths

Standardised procedures

Haun et al. used highly standardised procedures and controls to ensure that all children and primates took part in exactly the same way. The behaviour adjustments recorded can be retested.

Cause is pinned down

We can be sure the changes in behaviour were a direct result of observing a peer demonstrating an alternative behaviour strategy.

Familiar peers

The peer was known to the participant, so the findings reflect conformity to someone familiar, and not a stranger.

中文理解：强弱各记三条
弱点三条：任务假（往洞里放球换巧克力，日常没人这么玩）、猿是圈养的（野生黑猩猩未必这样）、长期效果没测（换格子是一时还是一直，不知道）。
强度三条：流程标准化（三个物种走完全相同的流程）、因果清楚（变化只能来自看同伴示范）、同伴是熟人（贴近真实的从众场景）。各配一句后果，就是一段完整评价。

14. Application

One important application of this research: it can be used to promote positive behaviour change in others.

  • Knowing that peers are a source of social influence that mediates behaviour change can be used to encourage compliant behaviours.
  • Specific behaviour change can be encouraged through peers, such as picking up litter or turning off electrical appliances.
  • Feedback in social terms works too: if we know we are consuming more electricity than our neighbours, it may influence household owners to reduce energy consumption themselves.

The textbook's own scenario (Thinking like a psychologist)

Imagine you have been tasked with promoting a uniform code at your school. Using Haun et al.'s research, consider ways that you would encourage students to adhere to the correct school uniform. The logic of the study points to one lever: make the desired behaviour something students see their peers doing.

17. Exam Lab

The question below comes from the textbook's exam practice for this chapter.

Q1 — Describe the procedure used in Haun et al.'s (2014) study. (3 marks)

Three marks, so build the answer as three stages.

Firstly, each participant learned that only one colour section of the three-section box dispensed a reward, to a criterion of dropping the ball into the correct section eight out of ten times. Secondly, they watched three familiar conspecific peers (demonstrators) each place the ball in a differently coloured, equally effective section twice, producing six demonstrations of the majority strategy. Thirdly, after 30 seconds the participant was given three balls, one at a time, with all choices rewarded, and their video-recorded choices were coded by two independent observers as 'switch', 'stay' or 'other response'.

Q2 — Describe the aim of this contemporary study. (from the chapter checkpoint)

To investigate the tendencies of human and non-human animals to adjust their behaviour to a majority of peers in a similar situation. Specifically, whether two-year-old children and primates (chimpanzees and orangutans) would adjust an individually acquired behaviour strategy after exposure to a majority of peers displaying an equally effective but different strategy.

Q3 — Explain one weakness of Haun et al.'s study.

Conformity was tested with an artificial task: watching a conspecific place a ball in a hole for a reward is not a typical everyday activity. The behaviour change was demonstrated only under contrived conditions, so the findings may not reflect how people conform to peers in everyday situations such as the playground, limiting the ecological validity of the conclusions.

18. Key Terms

conspecific
A member of one's own species; each participant watched three familiar conspecific peers.
demonstrator
A trained peer who shows the majority strategy; three demonstrators dropped the ball twice each.
switch
Choosing the majority response, the strategy the demonstrators used.
stay
Keeping their own previously learned preference.
other response
Choosing the third section, neither preferred nor demonstrated; coded by two independent observers.
learning criterion
Dropping the ball into the rewarding section eight out of ten times before the demonstration phase.
individually acquired strategy
A behaviour learned alone before peer exposure; the baseline against which switching is measured.
equally effective strategy
The majority's way worked just as well, so switching has no practical benefit; any change is social influence.
conformity score
Switch minus stay; −3 means total loyalty to the learned strategy, +3 means total conformity.
captive primates
Apes living in captivity; their behaviour may not reflect wild chimpanzees and orangutans.
standardised procedure
All children and primates took part in exactly the same way, so results can be retested.
application
Peers mediate behaviour change; used to promote litter picking, switching off appliances, or cutting energy use relative to neighbours.
