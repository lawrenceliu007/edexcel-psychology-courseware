📚 Learning Objectives & Syllabus Map

⚡ Quick Summary

Chapter 19 is the Practical Investigation chapter in Topic C: Biological Psychology. You must design and conduct your own correlational study (a study that measures the relationship between two co-variables) on aggression or body rhythms. The exam does not ask you to do the practical in the exam room, but it tests whether you actually did one — answers that are too generic score 0 marks.

5 syllabus learning objectives (p.216)

  1. Design and conduct a correlational study on aggression or body rhythms.
  2. Make design decisions when planning and gathering data.
  3. Analyse correlational data including conducting an inferential test for a relationship (Spearman's rank).
  4. Evaluate strengths and weaknesses of the correlation and suggest improvements.
  5. Write up the procedure, results and discussion section of a report.

🎯 Exam Key Points

  • The exam does not ask "write a brand new practical investigation." It asks you to describe your own practical (procedure / findings / validity improvements) and read other people's scenarios (e.g. WPS02/01 Q2 Hikaru) and pick holes in their design.
  • Mark scheme wording (WPS01/01 Q3): "Answers must relate to their practical. Generic answers score 0 marks." So memorise your own sample, sample size, sampling method, materials.
  • Your practical is treated by the examiner exactly like a named study: they expect Aim · Method · Sample · Procedure · Results · Conclusion · Evaluation. Use AMSPRC as the skeleton.
  • You must do a Spearman's rank test (Ch17 gives the mechanics) and write a conclusion sentence with 7 mandatory ingredients (see §7).

What this chapter will give you

You will leave this chapter with:

  • A pick-and-run practical: pick from 9 syllabus-approved topics (5 aggression + 4 body rhythms) and follow the worked example.
  • The textbook example (masculinity × aggression, n=10, rₛ = 0.318) fully walked through, so if you haven't done your own yet, you can write up this one.
  • A Spearman's rank scaffold that connects directly to Ch17 (you already know the formula).
  • A report template (procedure / results / discussion) plus model answers for the 3 standard exam questions.

🎯 Aim, Hypothesis & Variables

⚡ Quick Summary

A correlational study measures the relationship between two co-variables (both are measured, neither is manipulated). You start with a research question, turn it into an aim, write an operationalised hypothesis (directional or non-directional), and choose a target population.

Step 1 — Pick your investigation

The syllabus (p.216) gives 9 suggested titles. Pick one you can actually run in your school (you'll be tested on the details, so it needs to be one you remember).

🟧 Aggression track (5 options)

  • Masculinity × aggression (the textbook example)
  • Height × self-reported aggression
  • Self-reported aggression × contact-sport injuries
  • Premenstrual tension × self-reported aggression
  • Age × self-reported aggression

🟦 Body rhythms track (4 options)

  • Age × sleep duration
  • Physical exercise × sleep quality
  • Severity of premenstrual tension × hours of sleep
  • Sleep quality × unhealthy snacking
  • Social media time × sleep duration

Step 2 — Turn the title into an aim

Worked example (textbook p.216)

Research question: "Is there a link between the masculinity of a person and aggressive behaviour?"

Aim: "To investigate whether there is a link between the masculinity of a person and aggressive behaviour."

💡 理解小贴士
Aim = research question 用学术腔再说一遍。回答里必须出现 "to investigate whether..." 这个固定句式,阅卷一眼就认出结构化答案。

Step 3 — Hypotheses

TypeFormatWhen to use
Alternative (H₁)"There will be a significant positive/negative correlation between X and Y."You predict the direction (sign) of the relationship.
Null (H₀)"There will be no relationship between X and Y. Any relationship found will be due to chance."Always written alongside H₁, regardless of direction.
Directional"positive correlation between masculinity and aggression"Previous research or theory gives a clear prediction (textbook uses this).
Non-directional"a relationship (could be positive or negative) between X and Y"No clear prior — write the hypothesis without specifying sign, then run a two-tailed Spearman's test.
Operationalised alternative hypothesis (textbook p.217)

"There will be a significant positive correlation between a participant's masculinity score on a quiz and their scores on a moderately violent video game."

This rewrites the abstract concept "masculinity" as "score on the quiz" and "aggression" as "score on the video game" — that is operationalisation.

Step 4 — Variables checklist

  • Co-variable 1 (IV of correlational design): measure of X (e.g. masculinity score 0–100).
  • Co-variable 2 (DV of correlational design): measure of Y (e.g. aggression score 0–20).
  • Target population: the wider group you want to generalise to (e.g. "the male general public").
  • Both variables must be at least ordinal level of measurement (rank-able), otherwise Spearman's rank won't work.

🎯 Exam Tip — avoid these slips

  • Don't call them IV and DV — they are co-variables. The exam mark scheme will downmark if you write "independent variable" for a correlation.
  • Always write the null hypothesis even if it feels obvious. The mark scheme specifically asks for both.
  • If your hypothesis is directional, run a one-tailed Spearman's test (textbook does this). If non-directional, run a two-tailed test.

🔬 Design, Controls & Sampling

⚡ Quick Summary

A correlational design measures two variables for the same participants — it does not manipulate anything. You still need standardised controls (same instructions, same testing conditions) and a sampling method you can defend at exam time. The textbook uses an opportunity sample of n = 10 males, aged 19–52.

Design choice — why correlation, not experiment?

ExperimentCorrelation (this chapter)
Manipulates a variable?Yes (IV)No — both measured
Can establish cause?Yes (with controls)No — third variable problem
Direction of relationship?IV → DVX ↔ Y (symmetric)
Test used in this topic—Spearman's rank
Design typeRepeated / independent measuresRepeated measures (both vars recorded for every participant)

Controls you must put in place (textbook p.217)

  • Same instructions for every participant — written, standardised, read aloud identically.
  • Same testing environment — same room, same time of day, similar noise level.
  • Objective measurement — use computerised tests where possible (masculinity quiz is computer-marked; video game score is logged by software).
  • Equal proficiency at the task — give everyone the same practice session (textbook: 3-minute practice before the 5-minute data-gathering run).
  • Eliminate extraneous variables — silence during the quiz; participants tested individually or in matched groups.

Sampling — which method and what to write

Textbook choice (p.217-218)

Method: opportunity sample of 10 male participants, recruited by advertising on the university/college noticeboard (so technically a volunteer sample through opportunity channels).

Why this size? "about ten participants" — small but enough for Spearman's rank at p<0.05 (critical value 0.564).

Weakness to flag at exam time: not generalisable to the wider male population because (a) small n, (b) volunteer bias (volunteers tend to be more compliant), (c) all from one institution (ethnocentric, Western industrialised culture).

Sampling methodStrength for your exam answerWeakness to acknowledge
OpportunityQuick, easy, realistic for school settingUnrepresentative; only people available at the time
VolunteerParticipants are willing, fewer drop-outsSelf-selection bias — more compliant / more opinionated
RandomEvery member of target population has equal chance → generalisableHard to do in a school; may produce uneven group sizes
StratifiedEnsures subgroups (e.g. year group) are representedNeed to know population proportions first; complex to organise
💡 理解小贴士
写抽样 weakness 时不要只说 "small sample" — 一定要把 weakness 链到 generalisability 或 representativeness 上,例如 "因为是 opportunity sample,所以样本只代表了这所学校的男生,无法推广到所有 18 岁以上男性。" 这才是 AO3 的得分点。

📜 Ethics & Consent Form

⚡ Quick Summary

You need to deal with ethics before participants start. Get written informed consent, tell them the true nature of the study (not just a vague cover story), guarantee withdrawal at any point, keep data confidential and anonymous, and pilot your materials on a friend first to surface issues you missed.

Consent form — the 7 mandatory items (textbook p.218)

  1. Aim(s) of the study — what you are investigating.
  2. Exactly what they will be expected to do — full detail so they can decide.
  3. Any possible implications — e.g. they will play an aggressive video game.
  4. Right to withdraw at any point without consequence (and can take their data with them).
  5. Anonymisation & destruction of data — names replaced with numbers, data destroyed after a set date.
  6. Who else will see the findings and how the information will be distributed.
  7. Contact details — researcher + supervising teacher / school / college, plus signatures of researcher and participant.

Special considerations for aggression studies

  • No one under 16 — the video game may be age-inappropriate.
  • Game must be age-appropriate for the sample.
  • All participants must be informed in advance that they will be expected to play a violent game.
  • Protection from psychological harm — debrief must include a chance to discuss any discomfort.

🎯 Exam Tip

When asked "explain one ethical issue Hikaru should have considered" (WPS02/01 Q2c, 2 marks), pick one specific BPS principle and link it to the study: e.g. "Informed consent — participants should have been told before taking part that the food given would be measured against aggression scores, so they could decide whether to take part." The mark scheme rewards: principle + application + link to study.

📋 Procedure & Example Report

⚡ Quick Summary

The textbook example is a 2-hour lab session: 10 males, 19–52 years old. They sit at numbered computers in silence for a masculinity quiz (25–42 min), wait in a lounge, then play a 3-min practice + 5-min data run on a bespoke moderately-aggressive video game, scored automatically. They are debriefed individually or in pairs.

Step-by-step procedure (textbook p.219)

  1. Briefing & informed consent — researcher reads standard briefing, participants sign consent forms, allocate a participant number.
  2. Masculinity quiz — bank of computers in IT room; silence; supervised; computer records the score against the participant number.
  3. Lounge wait — refreshments; participants wait until called.
  4. Video game — individually in one of two small lab rooms; 3-min practice then 5-min data run; laptop preloaded with bespoke game; research assistant sets up and logs score; minimal interaction.
  5. Debrief — in another small room, individually or in pairs; reminded of right to withdraw; questions answered; offered to see results when available.
  6. Thank & release — thanks, optional insights, dismissed.

🎯 Exam Tip — describe the procedure (4 marks)

The textbook exam question (p.226) asks for 4 marks. Mark scheme-style answer:

  1. Sample: "An opportunity sample of 10 male participants aged 19–52 was recruited via university noticeboard."
  2. Apparatus & scoring: "A computerised masculinity quiz (0–100) and a bespoke video game that logged aggression as characters knocked down."
  3. Design: "A repeated-measures correlational design — both co-variables recorded for every participant."
  4. Procedure: "Participants sat in silence at numbered computers for the quiz (25–42 min), waited in a lounge, then played a 3-min practice + 5-min data run on the video game. Standardised instructions were used; all sessions were supervised; participants were debriefed individually and reminded of their right to withdraw."

Writing the procedure for your own study

Use the textbook 8-point checklist (p.225):

  • Who was used, how many, from where, and the sampling technique.
  • The apparatus used and how data was scored.
  • The design (typically repeated measures for correlation).
  • The brief, standardised instructions and debrief.
  • The location and who was present.
  • Whether participants were tested individually or in groups.
  • What the participants did during the investigation (procedure, timings).
  • Controls put into place.

Spearman's rank — 7-step recipe (textbook p.220-221)

  1. Rank scores on co-variable A (1 = smallest).
  2. Rank scores on co-variable B the same way.
  3. Calculate the difference d between the ranks for each participant.
  4. Square each difference to get d².
  5. Sum the squared differences: Σd².
  6. Count the number of participants n.
  7. Plug into the formula.
Spearman's rank formula

rs = 1 − (6 × Σd²) ÷ (n(n² − 1))

Applied to the textbook data:

rs = 1 − (6 × 112.5) ÷ (10 × (10² − 1)) = 1 − 675 ÷ 990 = 1 − 0.682 = 0.318

💡 理解小贴士
Σd² = 112.5 这个数字课本里有 3 个相同的 scores(10 出现两次, 15 出现两次, 17 出现两次),排名时要用"位置平均值"——例如两个 10 排第 2、第 3 位,就都给 rank 2.5。这是 tied ranks 的标准处理,Ch17 Spearman 工作簿里有完整练习。

Handling tied ranks

Number position12345678910
Score for B (ascending)6101014151516171718
Rank for B12.52.545.55.578.58.510

Two equal scores → average the number positions they occupy, then skip the next rank accordingly. e.g. 10s at positions 2 and 3 → both get (2+3)/2 = 2.5; the next score (14) gets rank 4 (not 3).

⚖️ Significance, Validity, Reliability & Generalisability

⚡ Quick Summary

After you compute rₛ, compare it with the critical value for your n at p<0.05. If rₛ < critical value → accept null hypothesis. Then evaluate your study on three textbook axes: validity, reliability, generalisability — each with the textbook's actual critique ready to deploy.

Worked significance decision (textbook)

rₛ calculated = 0.318; n = 10; one-tailed test at p < 0.05 → critical value = 0.564.

0.318 < 0.564 → calculated value is less than critical value → not significant → accept null hypothesis.

Drawing conclusions — the 7 mandatory ingredients (textbook EXAM TIP p.223)

When writing a statistical conclusion, the exam mark scheme expects all 7 of these:

  1. The calculated value (e.g. "rₛ = 0.318").
  2. The critical value (e.g. "0.564").
  3. The probability / level of significance (e.g. "p < 0.05").
  4. The number of participants (e.g. "n = 10").
  5. Whether it was a one-tailed or two-tailed test.
  6. Whether the calculated value was greater than, less than, or equal to the critical value.
  7. What hypothesis is supported (restate it).

🎯 Model statistical conclusion sentence (textbook p.223)

"The calculated value of the Spearman's rank test was r = 0.318. This was less than the critical value of 0.564 for a one-tailed test at p < 0.05 with n = 10. Therefore the result is not significant and the null hypothesis can be supported, which states that there will be no relationship between masculinity and aggression, and any relationship found was due to chance."

Validity — was the test a true measure?

Textbook critique (p.224)
  • Masculinity quiz — "very superficial, ignores many other factors that influence masculinity, such as social roles and norms of behaviour" → low face validity.
  • Video game aggression score — "lacking ecological validity" because hitting virtual characters does not necessarily translate to real-world aggression.
  • Could reflect gaming skill rather than aggression ("the number of times a character was hit was merely a reflection of the rules of the game").
  • Third variable problem — correlation cannot establish causation; another unknown variable (e.g. family environment) could affect both.

Reliability — would the test give the same result again?

Textbook critique (p.224-225)
  • Strength: Standardised procedure and objective measures (computer-marked quiz, auto-logged game score) → replicable.
  • Weakness: Order of tasks varied — some did the quiz first, some had a longer gap → not all participants at the same level of focused attention.
  • Weakness: Prior gaming experience varied → some had higher generic skill, not consistently measuring aggression.
  • If a questionnaire had been used to measure aggression, reliability would have been even lower (subjective interpretation).

Generalisability — can the findings leave the lab?

Textbook critique (p.225)
  • Small sample (n = 10) — low statistical power.
  • Volunteer sample — those who came forward may have a more compliant personality and could have altered their behaviour to meet expectations (demand characteristics).
  • Ethnocentric — sample from an industrialised Western culture; cultures differ in how aggression is nurtured or prohibited.
  • Single-sex (male only) — cannot explain female aggression.

✍️ Report Writing & Exam Practice

⚡ Quick Summary

The report has three sections: procedure (what you did), results (what you found), discussion (what it means + strengths/weaknesses/improvements). The textbook gives explicit checklists for each section. The exam tests these via 3 standard 2/4-mark questions.

The 3 textbook exam questions (p.226) + model answers

Q1 · Describe the procedure of your practical investigation in biological psychology (4 marks)

Mark scheme expects 4 distinct points. A model answer:

  1. Sample: "An opportunity sample of 10 male participants aged 19–52 was recruited via advertising on the university noticeboard."
  2. Apparatus / scoring: "A computerised masculinity quiz (scored 0–100) and a bespoke moderately-aggressive video game that logged aggression as the number of characters knocked down (0–20)."
  3. Design: "A repeated-measures correlational design — both co-variables were recorded for every participant."
  4. Procedure: "Participants sat in silence at numbered computers for the quiz (25–42 minutes), waited in a lounge, then played a 3-minute practice + 5-minute data run on the video game. Standardised instructions were read aloud; participants were supervised; debriefed individually and reminded of the right to withdraw."

Top band: Clear, detailed, sequential, includes sample size, location, timings.

Q2 · Explain whether the findings of your practical investigation were statistically significant (2 marks)

Model answer (textbook n=10, rₛ=0.318):

"The calculated value of Spearman's rank was rₛ = 0.318. This was less than the critical value of 0.564 for a one-tailed test at p < 0.05 with n = 10. Therefore the result was not statistically significant and the null hypothesis was supported, meaning any relationship found between masculinity and aggression could be due to chance."

Mark scheme: 1 mark for stating calculated vs critical value; 1 mark for correct decision (significant / not significant) + hypothesis supported.

Q3 · Explain one improvement that could be made to the validity of your practical investigation (2 marks)

Model answer A (ecological validity): "The video game lacked ecological validity because hitting virtual characters does not translate to real-world aggressive behaviour. An improvement would be to use a more naturalistic measure of aggression, such as the Rosenzweig Picture-Frustration Test where participants respond to frustrating scenarios and aggressive responses are coded by a blind rater."

Model answer B (face validity of masculinity): "The masculinity quiz was superficial and ignored social roles and norms. An improvement would be to use a more comprehensive masculinity scale that includes behavioural items (e.g. risk-taking, help-seeking refusal) as well as self-report attitudes."

Mark scheme: 1 mark for identifying a validity weakness; 1 mark for explaining an improvement linked to that weakness.

Discussion section — what to include (textbook p.225-226)

  • Conclusions drawn from the data analysis.
  • Findings explained within the wider context of background theories and previous research.
  • Limitations and strengths of the investigation.
  • Possible improvements to the methodology.
  • Practical uses of the findings and potential implications for organisations, education, clinical practice or society.

Improvement checklist (textbook p.226)

Question to askLinked improvement
Was the sample representative?If not — use random or stratified sampling to broaden.
Was the procedure standardised?If not — write a standardised script, give identical time slots.
Were there any control issues?If yes — add controls (same time of day, same room, same practice length).
Were the variables operationalised effectively?If no — use a more valid measure (e.g. real aggressive behaviour, validated scale).
Was data collection / analysis subjective?If yes — use blind marking, computerised scoring, two independent raters.
Were there unforeseen ethical issues?If yes — revise consent form, add debrief, tighten confidentiality.

🎯 Textbook EXAM TIP (p.226) — read this 3 times

"Your practical investigation is like any other classic or contemporary study you have learned, so you need to know the aims, procedure, results, conclusions and improvements in addition to strengths and weaknesses. You could be asked to write an essay evaluating your practical investigation, so this should be prepared in the same way as preparing for evaluating a classic or contemporary study."

Treat your practical as the named study for any Unit 2 question that says "your practical investigation". AMSPRC skeleton + 3 evaluation axes (validity, reliability, generalisability) = full marks.

Key Terms Glossary

TermDefinition
CorrelationA statistical technique measuring the strength and direction of a relationship between two co-variables.
Co-variableOne of the two variables measured in a correlational study. Neither is independent or dependent.
OperationalisationDefining an abstract concept in terms of how it will be concretely measured.
Directional hypothesisPredicts the specific nature (sign) of the relationship — paired with a one-tailed test.
Non-directional hypothesisPredicts a relationship but not its sign — paired with a two-tailed test.
Null hypothesis (H₀)States there is no relationship between the co-variables; any finding is due to chance.
Alternative hypothesis (H₁)States there is a significant relationship between the co-variables.
Spearman's rank (rₛ)Non-parametric test of correlation for ordinal data; produces a coefficient between −1 and +1.
Critical valueThe value of rₛ that must be met or exceeded for the result to be significant at the chosen p level.
p < 0.05Conventional significance threshold — less than a 5% probability the result is due to chance.
One-tailed testTests for a relationship in one predicted direction only.
Two-tailed testTests for a relationship in either direction (used with non-directional hypotheses).
Third variable problemAn unmeasured variable that influences both co-variables, producing a spurious correlation.
Ecological validityThe extent to which findings generalise to real-life settings.
Volunteer / opportunity sampleNon-random sampling — participants self-select (volunteer) or are chosen because they are available (opportunity).
Pilot studyA small-scale trial run of the procedure to check instructions, timing, venue and ethics.
Demand characteristicsCues in the study that lead participants to guess the aim and change behaviour accordingly.
EthnocentricResearch that assumes the findings of one culture apply universally; biased toward the researcher's own culture.
