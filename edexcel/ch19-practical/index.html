Chapter 19 — Practical Investigation in Biological Psychology

A 7-step workflow for designing, conducting, analysing & evaluating your own correlational study on aggression or body rhythms
Topic C · Biological Psychology Correlational Design Spearman's Rank Workflow + Diagnose

📋 Step 0 — What you will produce

⚡ Quick Summary

This chapter is a workflow, not a reference document. You will follow 7 steps from picking a topic through to writing your report. At each step you will (a) complete the textbook action, (b) diagnose Hikaru — a real exam scenario where the design has weaknesses you must spot, and (c) try the same action on your own chosen study.

Spec 3.4 — what the exam requires you to cover

The Edexcel IAL Psychology specification (Issue 5, Feb 2021, p.30) sets the following musts for Topic C's practical investigation:

  1. Design and conduct a correlational study with descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, range) and a non-parametric test of difference / correlation.
  2. Make design decisions: choose co-variables, operationalise them, address ethics, formulate a hypothesis, put controls in place.
  3. Present findings with a scatter diagram.
  4. Conduct a Spearman's rank test and decide on statistical significance.
  5. Evaluate validity, reliability and generalisability.
  6. Write up the procedure, results and discussion sections of a report.

The 5 syllabus learning objectives (textbook p.216)

  1. Design and conduct a correlational study on aggression or body rhythms.
  2. Make design decisions when planning and gathering data.
  3. Analyse correlational data including an inferential test for a relationship.
  4. Evaluate strengths and weaknesses of the correlation and suggest improvements.
  5. Write up the procedure, results and discussion section of a report on your investigation.

🎯 Exam Key Points

  • No "design from scratch" question. Every practical question gives a scenario (Hikaru, Cyr, etc.); you patch the design or evaluate it. So diagnostic ability is what the exam actually tests.
  • No need to bring notes. The exam is closed-book, but mark scheme wording for Unit 1 ("Answers must relate to their practical") means you must remember your own design as a reference point when diagnosing scenarios.
  • Treat your practical like a named study — examiner expects Aim · Method · Sample · Procedure · Results · Conclusion · Evaluation (AMSPRC skeleton).
  • Spearman's rank scaffold lives in Ch17; this chapter feeds it your own data.

🎯 Step 1 — Aim, hypothesis & variables

⚡ Quick Summary

A correlational study measures the relationship between two co-variables — both are recorded, neither is manipulated. You start with a research question, turn it into an aim, write an operationalised hypothesis, and identify a target population. Spec must 2 starts here.

1

Pick the topic you will commit to

Don't pick the "easiest" topic — pick one you can actually run in your school and one you'll remember in two years. You will be tested on details of your practical, so familiarity beats cleverness.

2

Write the aim

Use the fixed phrase "To investigate whether there is a link between X and Y." The marker recognises the structure immediately.

Textbook example (p.216): "To investigate whether there is a link between the masculinity of a person and aggressive behaviour."

3

Write both hypotheses

TypeFormatPairs with
Alternative (H₁)"There will be a significant positive/negative correlation between X and Y."Directional prediction → one-tailed test
Null (H₀)"There will be no relationship between X and Y. Any relationship found will be due to chance."Always written, regardless of direction
Non-directional H₁"There will be a significant correlation (positive or negative) between X and Y."No clear prior → two-tailed test

Worked example (textbook p.217): "There will be a significant positive correlation between a participant's masculinity score on a quiz and their scores on a moderately violent video game." — directional because prior literature predicts higher masculinity → higher aggression.

4

Operationalise both co-variables

Turn abstract concepts into measurable scores. Spearman's rank only works on ordinal data (or higher ranked down to ordinal).

Textbook operationalisation:

  • Masculinity → score 0–100 on a computerised masculinity quiz.
  • Aggression → number of characters knocked down (1 point each) in a moderately violent video game.
💡 关键提醒
Don't call them independent variable and dependent variable — they are co-variables. The Edexcel mark scheme downmarks if you write "IV" / "DV" for a correlation.

🔬 Step 2 — Design decisions (sampling, ethics, controls, pilot)

⚡ Quick Summary

You are not manipulating anything (it's a correlation), but you still need a defensible sampling method, an ethics pack, and controls. The textbook uses an opportunity/volunteer sample of n=10 — quick but weak on generalisability. Spec must 2 is fully covered here.

5

Choose a sampling method and defend it

Sampling methodStrengthWeakness to acknowledge
OpportunityQuick, easy, realistic for schoolUnrepresentative; only people available
VolunteerParticipants willing, fewer drop-outsSelf-selection bias (more compliant / opinionated)
RandomEvery member equal chance → generalisableHard to do in school
StratifiedSubgroups represented proportionallyNeed population proportions first

Textbook example (p.217): opportunity sample of 10 male participants aged 19–52, recruited by advertising on a university noticeboard. Bias explicitly acknowledged: not generalisable due to (a) small n, (b) volunteer bias, (c) Western industrialised, male-only.

6

Write the consent form — 7 mandatory items (p.218)

  1. Aim(s) of the study.
  2. Exactly what they will be expected to do — in full detail so they can decide.
  3. Any possible implications — e.g. playing a moderately aggressive game.
  4. Right to withdraw at any point without consequence + option to take their data with them.
  5. Anonymisation & destruction — names replaced with numbers, data destroyed after a set date.
  6. Who else will see the findings and how information will be distributed.
  7. Contact details (researcher + supervisor) and signatures of researcher and participant.
7

Put controls in place (textbook p.217)

  • Same standardised instructions for every participant (written, read aloud identically).
  • Same testing environment — same room, same time of day, similar noise.
  • Objective measurement — computerised tests where possible.
  • Equal proficiency at the task — same practice session length for everyone.
  • Eliminate extraneous variables — silence during the quiz; matched groups if testing together.

Special rule for aggression studies: no one under 16; the game must be age-appropriate; participants must be told in advance they will play a violent game.

8

Run a pilot study

A pilot is a dry run on 1–3 friends. It is NOT a validity or reliability check — it surfaces timing, instructions, venue, ethical problems before you commit your real sample.

💡 关键提醒
When asked "explain one sampling weakness", don't just say "small sample". Link it: "opportunity sample → only represents students at this school → findings cannot be generalised to the wider male population." That linkage is where AO3 marks come from.

📋 Step 3 — Materials & procedure

⚡ Quick Summary

Design the measurement tools (questionnaire, rating scale, computer test, video game), write standardised instructions, then write the 6-step procedure. Spec must 6 (write-up) starts being assembled here — the procedure section is built from these steps.

9

Choose measurement tools

For a correlational study both co-variables must be at least ordinal. Typical tools:

  • Questionnaire / rating scale (e.g. 5-point Likert: 1 = never, 5 = always) — easy to run with many participants, but subjective.
  • Computerised quiz (e.g. masculinity quiz) — objective, computer-marked, harder to design.
  • Behavioural measure (e.g. video game score, reaction time, hot-sauce paradigm) — objective, but may not reflect real-life behaviour (ecological validity).
10

Write standardised instructions

One script, read aloud identically to every participant. Cover: what they will do, how long it takes, that they can stop at any time, that their data is anonymous.

11

Write the 6-step procedure (textbook p.219)

  1. Briefing & informed consent — researcher reads standard briefing; participants sign consent forms; allocate a participant number.
  2. First measurement (e.g. masculinity quiz in IT room, silence, computer-marked).
  3. Waiting period — refreshments; participants wait until called.
  4. Second measurement (e.g. video game in lab room, 3-min practice + 5-min data run).
  5. Debrief — in another small room, individually or in pairs; reminded of right to withdraw; questions answered; offered to see results when available.
  6. Thank & release — thanks, optional insights, dismissed.
💡 关键提醒
Use repeated measures — both co-variables recorded for every participant. Don't call it "matched pairs" (that's for experiments) or "matched samples" (that's for quasi-experiments).

🎯 How this feeds the 4-mark "Describe the procedure" question

For full marks, your procedure description must mention all 4:

  1. Sample — who, how many, from where, sampling technique.
  2. Apparatus & scoring — what tools, how data is scored.
  3. Design — repeated-measures correlational.
  4. Procedure — what participants did, in sequence, with timings.

15

Run Spearman's rank — the 7-step recipe (p.220-221)

  1. Rank scores on co-variable A (1 = smallest).
  2. Rank scores on co-variable B the same way.
  3. Calculate difference d between the ranks for each participant.
  4. Square each difference to get d².
  5. Sum the squared differences: Σd².
  6. Count n.
  7. Plug into the formula.
Spearman's rank formula

rs = 1 − (6 × Σd²) ÷ (n(n² − 1))

Applied to the textbook data:

rs = 1 − (6 × 112.5) ÷ (10 × (10² − 1)) = 1 − 675 ÷ 990 = 1 − 0.682 = 0.318

Tied ranks: if two scores are equal (e.g. two 10s), average the number positions they occupy. e.g. 10s at positions 2 and 3 → both get rank 2.5; the next score gets rank 4 (skip 3).

Worked decision: calculated rₛ = 0.318; critical value (one-tailed, p<0.05, n=10) = 0.564. Since 0.318 < 0.564, the result is not significant → accept null hypothesis.

🎯 EXAM TIP — the 7 mandatory ingredients of a statistical conclusion (p.223)

  1. The calculated value (e.g. "rₛ = 0.318").
  2. The critical value (e.g. "0.564").
  3. The probability / level of significance (e.g. "p < 0.05").
  4. The number of participants (e.g. "n = 10").
  5. Whether it was a one-tailed or two-tailed test.
  6. Whether the calculated value was greater than, less than, or equal to the critical value.
  7. What hypothesis is supported (restate it).

Model sentence: "The calculated value of the Spearman's rank test was rₛ = 0.318. This was less than the critical value of 0.564 for a one-tailed test at p < 0.05 with n = 10. Therefore the result is not significant and the null hypothesis can be supported, which states that there will be no relationship between masculinity and aggression, and any relationship found was due to chance."

⚖️ Step 5 — Evaluate (Validity · Reliability · Generalisability)

⚡ Quick Summary

Spec must 5: evaluate your study on three axes. For each axis, you need (a) what the textbook actually criticised in the worked example, (b) why it matters for AO3 marks, and (c) how to phrase it as a 2-mark exam answer.

17

VValidity — was the test a true measure?

Textbook critique (p.224)
  • Face validity of masculinity quiz — "very superficial, ignores many other factors that influence masculinity, such as social roles and norms of behaviour".
  • Ecological validity of video game — "hitting virtual characters does not necessarily translate into real-world aggressive tendencies, which are likely to be constrained by social regulations".
  • Concurrent validity — "the number of times a character was hit in the game was merely a reflection of the rules of the video game and not an indication of aggression" → could be measuring gaming skill.
  • Third variable problem — correlation cannot establish causation; an unknown variable (e.g. family environment, personality) could affect both.
18

RReliability — would the test give the same result again?

Textbook critique (p.224-225)
  • Strength: Standardised procedure and objective measures (computer-marked quiz, auto-logged game score) → replicable.
  • Weakness — order effects: Some did the quiz first; some had a longer gap before the game → different levels of attention.
  • Weakness — individual differences: Prior gaming experience varied → some had higher generic skill, not consistently measuring aggression.
  • If questionnaire used instead of behavioural measure, subjective interpretation would further reduce reliability.
19

GGeneralisability — can the findings leave the lab?

Textbook critique (p.225)
  • Small n = 10 — low statistical power, hard to detect a real effect.
  • Volunteer sample — those who came forward may have a more compliant personality and could alter behaviour to meet expectations (demand characteristics).
  • Ethnocentric — sample from an industrialised Western culture; cultures differ in how aggression is nurtured or prohibited.
  • Single-sex (male only) — cannot explain female aggression.

VValidity

"Were the variables a true measure of the concepts?"

Quiz is superficial; game measures gaming skill not real aggression; correlation cannot establish cause (third variable).

RReliability

"Were the procedures consistent?"

Standardised; computer-marked. But: order effects, varied gap between tasks, varied prior gaming experience.

GGeneralisability

"Can we apply this beyond the sample?"

Small n = 10; volunteer bias; Western industrialised; male-only → cannot apply to women or other cultures.

🛠️ Step 6 — Suggest improvements

⚡ Quick Summary

Every weakness needs a linked improvement. The textbook gives a 6-question improvement checklist (p.226). Spec must 5 is fully covered when each V/R/G weakness is paired with an improvement.

20

Textbook improvement checklist (p.226)

Question to askLinked improvement
Was the sample representative?If not — use random or stratified sampling to broaden.
Was the procedure standardised?If not — write a standardised script, give identical time slots.
Were there any control issues?If yes — add controls (same time of day, same room, same practice length).
Were the variables operationalised effectively?If no — use a more valid measure (e.g. real aggressive behaviour, validated scale).
Was data collection / analysis subjective?If yes — use blind marking, computerised scoring, two independent raters.
Were there unforeseen ethical issues?If yes — revise consent form, add debrief, tighten confidentiality.
21

Model validity-improvement answers (textbook p.226)

Answer A — ecological validity of the game

The video game lacked ecological validity because hitting virtual characters does not translate to real-world aggressive behaviour. An improvement would be to use a more naturalistic measure of aggression, such as the Rosenzweig Picture-Frustration Test where participants respond to frustrating scenarios and aggressive responses are coded by a blind rater.

Answer B — face validity of the masculinity quiz

The masculinity quiz was superficial and ignored social roles and norms. An improvement would be to use a more comprehensive masculinity scale that includes behavioural items (e.g. risk-taking, help-seeking refusal) as well as self-report attitudes.

Mark scheme (2 marks): 1 mark for identifying a validity weakness; 1 mark for explaining an improvement linked to that weakness.

💡 关键提醒
An "improvement" that doesn't address a specific weakness scores 0. Always write: weakness → improvement → WHY this fixes it. The "why" is the second mark.

✍️ Step 7 — Report writing

⚡ Quick Summary

Spec must 6: report has three sections — procedure (what you did), results (what you found), discussion (what it means + strengths/weaknesses/improvements). The textbook gives explicit checklists for each. The exam tests these via 3 standard 2/4-mark questions.

22

Write the Procedure section (textbook p.225 checklist)

  • Sample: who was used, how many, from where, sampling technique.
  • Apparatus: what was used and how data was scored.
  • Design: typically repeated measures for a correlation.
  • Standardised brief and debrief: what participants were told, what they were told after.
  • Location & presence: where the study took place and who was in the room.
  • Individual or group testing: state explicitly.
  • Procedure & timings: what participants did, in sequence, with time taken.
  • Controls: what was kept the same to reduce extraneous variables.
23

Write the Results section

  • Raw data table (Table 19.1 style).
  • Descriptive statistics (Table 19.2 style).
  • Scatter diagram (Figure 19.1 style) + interpretation of strength and direction.
  • Spearman's rank calculation + statement of statistical significance (with all 7 mandatory ingredients).
24

Write the Discussion section

  • Conclusions drawn from the data analysis.
  • Findings explained within the wider context of background theories and previous research.
  • Limitations and strengths of the investigation (V/R/G).
  • Possible improvements to the methodology.
  • Practical uses of the findings and potential implications for organisations, education, clinical practice or society.

🎯 The 3 textbook exam questions (p.226) — model answers

Q1 · Describe the procedure of your practical investigation in biological psychology (4 marks)

Mark scheme expects 4 distinct points. Model answer:

  1. Sample: "An opportunity sample of 10 male participants aged 19–52 was recruited via advertising on the university noticeboard."
  2. Apparatus / scoring: "A computerised masculinity quiz (scored 0–100) and a bespoke moderately-aggressive video game that logged aggression as the number of characters knocked down (0–20)."
  3. Design: "A repeated-measures correlational design — both co-variables were recorded for every participant."
  4. Procedure: "Participants sat in silence at numbered computers for the quiz (25–42 minutes), waited in a lounge, then played a 3-minute practice + 5-minute data run on the video game. Standardised instructions were read aloud; all sessions were supervised; participants were debriefed individually and reminded of their right to withdraw."

Top band: Clear, detailed, sequential, includes sample size, location, timings.

Q2 · Explain whether the findings of your practical investigation were statistically significant (2 marks)

Model answer (textbook n=10, rₛ=0.318):

"The calculated value of Spearman's rank was rₛ = 0.318. This was less than the critical value of 0.564 for a one-tailed test at p < 0.05 with n = 10. Therefore the result was not statistically significant and the null hypothesis was supported, meaning any relationship found between masculinity and aggression could be due to chance."

Mark scheme: 1 mark for stating calculated vs critical value; 1 mark for correct decision (significant / not significant) + hypothesis supported.

Q3 · Explain one improvement that could be made to the validity of your practical investigation (2 marks)

Model answer A (ecological validity): "The video game lacked ecological validity because hitting virtual characters does not translate to real-world aggressive behaviour. An improvement would be to use a more naturalistic measure of aggression, such as the Rosenzweig Picture-Frustration Test where participants respond to frustrating scenarios and aggressive responses are coded by a blind rater."

Model answer B (face validity of masculinity): "The masculinity quiz was superficial and ignored social roles and norms. An improvement would be to use a more comprehensive masculinity scale that includes behavioural items (e.g. risk-taking, help-seeking refusal) as well as self-report attitudes."

Mark scheme: 1 mark for identifying a validity weakness; 1 mark for explaining an improvement linked to that weakness.

📖 Key Terms Glossary

TermDefinition
CorrelationA statistical technique measuring the strength and direction of a relationship between two co-variables.
Co-variableOne of the two variables measured in a correlational study. Neither is independent or dependent.
OperationalisationDefining an abstract concept in terms of how it will be concretely measured.
Directional hypothesisPredicts the specific nature (sign) of the relationship — paired with a one-tailed test.
Non-directional hypothesisPredicts a relationship but not its sign — paired with a two-tailed test.
Null hypothesis (H₀)States there is no relationship between the co-variables; any finding is due to chance.
Alternative hypothesis (H₁)States there is a significant relationship between the co-variables.
Spearman's rank (rₛ)Non-parametric test of correlation for ordinal data; produces a coefficient between −1 and +1.
Critical valueThe value of rₛ that must be met or exceeded for the result to be significant at the chosen p level.
p < 0.05Conventional significance threshold — less than a 5% probability the result is due to chance.
One-tailed testTests for a relationship in one predicted direction only.
Two-tailed testTests for a relationship in either direction (used with non-directional hypotheses).
Third variable problemAn unmeasured variable that influences both co-variables, producing a spurious correlation.
Ecological validityThe extent to which findings generalise to real-life settings.
Volunteer / opportunity sampleNon-random sampling — participants self-select (volunteer) or are chosen because they are available (opportunity).
Pilot studyA small-scale trial run of the procedure to check instructions, timing, venue and ethics.
Demand characteristicsCues in the study that lead participants to guess the aim and change behaviour accordingly.
EthnocentricResearch that assumes the findings of one culture apply universally; biased toward the researcher's own culture.
