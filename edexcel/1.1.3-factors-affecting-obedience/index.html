1.1.3 FACTORS AFFECTING OBEDIENCE
影响服从的因素

Social Psychology · individual differences, situation & culture（个体差异、情境与文化）
🧠 Three factor families 🔑 Personality · situation · culture 📖 Textbook: Chapter 1, pages 10–19

1. Getting Started

🎭 Same order, three outcomes Textbook activity

A manager gives the same instruction to three people: stay late tonight and finish the stock check. One person obeys at once. One refuses. One obeys in the shop and ignores the instruction once they are at home. The words never changed — so what did?

Three answers, and none of them cancels the others out. Each one points at a different family of factors: something about the person, something about the situation, something about the culture they grew up in. This chapter sorts the evidence for each family — and asks how firm that evidence really is.

这一节要解决的核心问题：理论说明服从通常是怎么发生的，但同一个人换个房间、换个国家，服从率就会改变。哪些变量在起作用，就是 factors affecting obedience。读下去时请带着一个判断习惯：每看到一项证据就问两句 —— 这项证据是用什么方法得到的？这个差异真的来自那个变量本身吗？很多题的分数就藏在这两问里。

2. Four Families of Factors

Milgram's participants were not identical machines: some went to the end of the shock scale and some refused. That is where the second half of the chapter starts. Obedience and dissent / resistance（异议与抵抗）depend on four families of factors.

The map for the rest of the chapter: every study you meet belongs in one of these boxes.

Keep the four apart

Individual differences means personality and gender. It is not the same as situation and it is not the same as culture. In an exam answer, naming the wrong family costs the mark even when the study you describe is correct.

四个盒子怎么记：问自己一句 "差异来自哪里" —— 来自人本身（性格、性别）→ individual differences；来自现场布置（权威离得远不远、有没有制服、几个人在催）→ situational；来自长大的环境（国家/文化对权威的看法）→ cultural。考试常见陷阱：把 "在两个不同国家做" 写成 situational —— 那是 cultural。

3. Individual Differences: The Authoritarian Personality

personality（人格） — a set of relatively stable psychological traits which can influence how we think and act.
authoritarian personality（权威主义人格） — a person who is typically submissive to authority, because they may have been raised in a strict household and punished for non-compliance.

How it is measured: the F-Scale

Theodor Adorno et al. (1950) devised the F-Scale (Fascism Scale)（F 量表）, a questionnaire used to detect the authoritarian personality.

fascism（法西斯主义） — a very nationalist and authoritarian position, intolerant of others' views, based on an extreme right-wing political position.

The authoritarian personality has been associated with higher levels of obedience, because people with this type tend to maintain social rules and order.

The evidence

StudyWhat was doneWhat was found
Milgram & Elms (1966) Compared the F-Scale scores of 20 obedient and 20 dissenting participants from Milgram's experiments. Obedient participants had a higher F-Scale score, indicating an authoritarian personality type, compared with the dissenters.
Dambrun & Vatiné (2010) A computer simulation / virtual environment replication of Milgram's procedure. Authoritarianism was linked to obedience: people with high authoritarian scores were less likely to withdraw from the study.

Why this evidence is weaker than it looks

  • The F-Scale is a self-reported measure（自我报告）— people describe themselves, and those answers can be shaped by how they want to be seen.
  • The link between authoritarianism and obedience is a correlation（相关）, so we cannot be sure that one causes the other. A third variable may be responsible.
  • Dambrun and Vatiné offered two readings of the same result: those participants were submissive to the authority of the experimenter, or they showed a desire to punish the failing learner — the design cannot tell these apart.
这一节最值钱的一句话："F-Scale is self-reported and the link is a correlation, so we cannot be sure it causes obedience." 只要是 questionnaire + 得出两个变量一起变，就套这句。它同时踩中两个常考的评价点（自我报告不可靠、相关不等于因果），比再写一段研究过程更有分。

4. Individual Differences: Locus of Control

locus of control（控制点） — Rotter's (1966) personality theory, which outlines two types:
  • internal locus of control — people believe they are responsible for their own actions and are less influenced by others.
  • external locus of control — people believe their behaviour is largely beyond their control and due to external factors, so they are more influenced by the people around them.

Milgram's responsibility disc

Milgram followed up 118 participants from Experiments 1–4, both obedient and disobedient, and asked them to judge who was responsible for the shocks. Three hands were moved around a round disc to show the proportionate responsibility of the experimenter, the teacher (themselves) and the learner.

Who was blamedDissenting participantsObedient participants
Themselves48%lower
The experimenter39%lower
The learner12%25%

Dissenters took more of the blame themselves; obedient people were more likely to displace blame onto someone else. Obedient participants also fit the pattern of an external locus of control: not only were they more influenced by the authority figure, they believed they were not responsible for what happened.

Blass (1991): the interaction

Albert Blass investigated the relationship between locus of control and how participants behaved in Milgram's research. He found that people with an external locus of control were more likely to obey the instruction to shock the learner, and were more likely to blame the experimenter. But he also pointed out that the situation had an important role to play: obedience should be considered as an interaction between situation and personality — some individuals with an external locus of control may be more susceptible to obedience in particular situations.

Why Blass matters more than the others

Every other study in this chapter tries to show that one factor wins. Blass refuses to pick a winner. That "both matter, and they combine" position is usually the strongest way to end an evaluation: it uses all the evidence instead of throwing half of it away.

5. Individual Differences: Gender

Most of Milgram's participants were male, but he ran one variation (Experiment 8) with 40 women. Earlier research suggested women were more compliant, yet traditional beliefs picture women as more empathetic and less aggressive, which would predict the opposite — so the two predictions cancel out.

StudyFinding about gender
Milgram (Experiment 8)Women were virtually identical to men in obedience (65%), with 27.5% breaking off at the 300-volt level. Obedient women showed much higher rated anxiety than obedient men — a pattern also seen in Burger's (2009) replication.
Sheridan & King (1972)A live puppy received genuine shocks. All 13 women delivered the maximum shock, compared with only about half of the men.
Blass (1999) reviewAcross a review of ten obedience experiments, obedience levels for men and women were consistent in nine of them.
Kilham & Mann (1974)A partial replication in Australia: women were far less obedient (16%) than men (40%) — the one exception in Blass's set.

What the textbook concludes

Obedience research has used different procedures and produced inconsistent outcomes. Rather than proving a difference, the pattern suggests there are few gender differences in obedience, despite traditional beliefs that women would be more compliant to authority.

In an answer, the gender evidence is strongest when you write it as two sides — a study that found a difference (Sheridan & King) and reviews that found none (Blass) — and then say why the results are hard to compare.

6. Situational Factors

social impact theory（社会影响理论） — Latané (1981): three factors determine the impact of an authority figure upon a target — strength, immediacy and number. These are features of the situation, and they predict the level of obedience observed.
FactorWhat it meansWhat it predicts
StrengthThe authority figure's legitimate status or power.The stronger they are seen to be, the more authority they exert over the target.
ImmediacyHow proximal / close they are to the target.Influence is greater when they are close than when there is distance.
NumberHow many authority figures there are relative to targets.More sources of influence relative to targets means greater influence.

Milgram's own test of these three

  • Distance acts as a buffer. Obedience rose and fell depending on whether the authority figure was close to or far from the target — most clearly in the telephonic instruction condition, where the experimenter gave orders by telephone from another room.
  • Legitimacy matters. Milgram claimed obedience could only be established when the authority figure was perceived to be legitimate. Obedience was found at Yale University, and it fell when the experiment moved to Bridgeport or was conducted by an ordinary man instead of a scientist.

Support from a partial replication

Wim Meeus and Quinten Raaijmakers (1986) tested whether participants would follow orders to be psychologically cruel to someone they believed was applying for a job. The experimenter instructed the participant to make the applicant nervous and disturb the test; because of this, the applicant failed and was not offered the job.

  • Experimenter close to the participant: more than 90% were cruel to the applicant.
  • Experimenter gave the orders and left the room: this fell to around 36%.

Proximity（距离近）is therefore an important situational factor in determining the level of obedience achieved — and the effect appears outside Milgram's laboratory too.

情境因素一句话定理：权威越近、越强、越多，服从越高。Meeus 与 Raaijmakers 的 90% → 36% 是最好用的数字：同一个实验者、同一句话，只是走了出房间，服从率直接砍三分之二。

7. Cultural Factors

Obedience varies across cultures because culture affects the way we perceive authority. Smith and Bond (1998) describe two broad types:

Individualistic cultures（个人主义文化）

People tend to behave more independently and tolerate dissent, which suggests they resist conformity or obedience.

Collectivistic cultures（集体主义文化）

People behave as a collective group based on interdependence and cooperation, meaning compliance is important for the stability of the group.

The obvious prediction is that collectivistic cultures will be more obedient and less tolerant of dissent. Thomas Blass (1999) reviewed obedience research carried out 35 years after Milgram's first studies, using procedures similar enough to compare.

Obedience research reviewed by Blass (1999): percentage of participants giving the maximum shock. The two blue rows used procedures that differed from Milgram's.

Why the table is harder to read than it looks

Procedure differences masquerading as culture differences

  • Ancona and Pareyson (1968), Italy: the maximum shock level was only 330 volts compared with Milgram's 450, and only student participants were used — a group Milgram actively avoided because of their compliant and competitive nature. 330 volts may simply have seemed less dangerous.
  • Burley and McGuiness (1977), UK: just 20 students and a maximum of 225 volts.
  • Milgram's own proximity studies found 73%, which is closer to Italy's 85% than his famous 65% is — so the comparison depends heavily on which condition you compare.

A modern, naturally occurring comparison

Carl-Benedikt Frey (2020) analysed the contact tracing data collected during lockdown in the COVID-19 pandemic. Collectivistic countries recorded less in-country mobility than individualistic countries, even though the countries compared had similarly stringent lockdown policies. Individualistic countries appear to have been more defiant of the rules — or at least less compliant.

Where this leaves us

The impact of culture on obedience is complex and unclear, and understanding it is hindered by a lack of research in certain cultures and by a lack of studies that can genuinely be compared country to country. This is why "culture affects obedience, so collectivistic cultures obey more" is a weak conclusion, while "the research does not let us isolate culture from procedure" is a strong one.

本节最有价值的句子："看起来是文化差异，其实可能是程序差异。" 意大利 85% 用的最高电压只有 330V（比 Milgram 少 120V）而且只招学生 —— 电压更低自然更容易按到底。写文化因素的答案时，先说理论预测（collectivistic 更服从），再说为什么数据支持不了（程序不一致），最后给结论（complex and unclear），比背书式地列九个国家好得多。

9. Exam Lab

🧭 The shape of almost every answer in this section

  • What: name the factor accurately — the family first (individual difference, situation, culture), then the specific factor and the study behind it.
  • Why: say why it counts — what the evidence shows, or what the evidence fails to show.
  • Limit: most of the evidence here is self-reported and correlational, so cause cannot be claimed. Adding that sentence is often the second mark.

A sentence that only names the factor earns one mark; the same sentence plus the reason earns two.

✍️ Practice 1 — 6 marks

(a) Explain one reason why personality may affect obedience.
(b) Explain two reasons why personality may not affect obedience.

Model answer

  • (a) Reason it may: people with an external locus of control believe their behaviour is caused by things outside themselves, so they take less responsibility for their actions and obey more. This is supported by Miller (1975), who found that "externals" obeyed a high-status experimenter more than a low-status one, while "internals" showed no difference.
  • (b) Reason 1: many studies find little or no difference between people with an external and an internal locus of control — Schutz (1985) found no difference in obedience to administering painful shocks, which suggests personality is not influential.
  • (b) Reason 2: it is difficult to separate personality from upbringing, gender and situational influences, so personality is hard to isolate as a variable and test scientifically; those other variables may be producing the result.

Why this scores: one mark is for identifying each reason and a second for justifying or exemplifying it. Every point above carries a study or an argument, not just a claim.

✍️ Practice 2 — 8 marks (planning only)

Assess whether individual differences (personality and gender) can influence obedience.

Half the marks come from describing the research accurately and half from weighing it up. Plan four paragraphs:

ParagraphWhat goes in it
1Personality: authoritarian personality and Adorno's F-Scale; Milgram and Elms (1966) found the 20 obedient participants scored higher on the F-Scale than the 20 who defied. Note the method — a questionnaire given afterwards, so the link is a correlation.
2Locus of control: Rotter's dimension of internals and externals; Miller (1975) found externals obeyed a high-status experimenter more, but Schutz (1985) found no difference — evidence on both sides is what an assessment needs.
3Gender: Exp 8 found women obeyed at a similar rate to men; Sheridan and King (1972) found a very high rate with real puppies; Blass's review of ten studies found no consistent difference, and Kilham and Mann found women less obedient than men in one sample.
4A conclusion that answers the question asked: can individual differences influence obedience? Weigh the mixed findings and say whether Blass's interactionist position — personality matters most when the situation leaves room for it — explains the evidence better than personality alone.

Two conclusions are available: the findings conflict, and almost all of them come from self-report measures. Either conclusion has to be argued, not merely stated.

✍️ Practice 3 — 12 marks (planning only)

To what extent do individual differences (personality and gender) influence obedience?

This shape of question wants the same content but a longer chain of reasoning. Aim for five paragraphs, and notice that the last one is what separates the top levels from the middle ones.

ParagraphWhat goes in it
1State the case for personality: authoritarian personality (Adorno's F-Scale, Milgram and Elms' 20 obedient versus 20 defiant participants) and locus of control (Miller's externals obeying a high-status experimenter).
2State the case against: Schutz found no difference; Dambrun and Vatiné (2010) used a virtual simulation and found a link in the opposite direction for men. The evidence points both ways.
3Gender: Exp 8, Sheridan and King, Blass's review of ten studies, and the Kilham and Mann exception.
4Method criticism running through all of it: the measures are self-report questionnaires, so answers may be affected by social desirability; the designs are correlational, so no cause can be claimed; and situational factors vary alongside personality, which makes personality hard to isolate.
5A sustained conclusion: to what extent? Take a position — for example that individual differences explain part of the variation but interact with the situation, exactly as the interactionist position argues — and defend it against the evidence you have just given.

The paragraph that weighs rather than lists is the one that earns the difference between a middling answer and a strong one.

🔑 Exam key points — what a strong answer contains ›
  1. Three families, and exam questions expect you to keep them apart: individual differences = personality and gender; that is not the same as situation, and it is not the same as culture.
  2. Personality has two named factors: authoritarian personality (Adorno's F-Scale) and locus of control (Rotter, internals and externals).
  3. Situational factors come from Latané's social impact theory: strength, immediacy and number; Meeus and Raaijmakers took obedience from over 90% down to around 36% by removing one of them.
  4. Culture compares individualistic with collectivistic cultures — but cross-cultural differences may be procedure differences in disguise, since studies used different maximum voltages and student-only samples.
  5. The two limitations that fit almost any study here: the data are self-reported, and the link found is a correlation, so cause cannot be claimed.
  6. Every factor needs a study behind it, and every study needs its finding — a named study with no result earns nothing.
