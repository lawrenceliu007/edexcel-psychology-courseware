1. Learning Objectives & How Section E is Examined（学习目标与考试形式）

⚡ Quick Summary — 本节要学什么

  1. Unit 4 的 Section E 是一道 20 分的 synoptic essay（综述题），全部围绕 issues and debates（议题与争论） 展开。
  2. 它的难点不是"背一个知识点"，而是把整个课程的知识跨主题调出来，去论证一个宏大的命题——例如"心理学算不算一门科学"。
  3. 本章把 spec 9.3 的 11 个议题逐个讲透，并教你怎么把它们组装成一篇拿高分的论文。

🎯 By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Explain why Section E exists and what "synoptic"（跨主题综合）means in practice
  • Recall and define all eleven issues and debates from spec 9.3.1–9.3.11
  • Draw evidence from across topics A–H (Social, Cognitive, Biological, Learning, Clinical) to support each debate
  • Plan and write a balanced 20-mark essay with the correct AO1 : AO3 ratio (8 : 12)
  • Recognise the difference between describing a debate (AO1) and assessing it (AO3)
  • Answer the official WPS04 Q11 question on psychology as a science at Level 4 standard

Where Section E sits in the paper

Unit 4 (WPS04/01) is a 2-hour, 96-mark paper made of five sections. Section E is the final section and carries the joint-highest mark of any single question on the paper:

Exam tip: Section E is synoptic（综述性的）. The examiner is not checking whether you remember Chapter 21. They are checking whether you can move freely across the whole qualification — Milgram from Social, Raine from Biological, Rosenhan from Clinical — and use that range to argue a position.
中文理解：Section E 就像一场"毕业论文答辩"。前面 Section A–D 像"单元测验"，考你某一章的掌握；Section E 考的是"你有没有把整门课学通"。题目通常很大——"心理学算不算科学？""心理学该不该用于社会控制？"——你要自己决定从哪几个主题搬证据、怎么搭论证结构、最后给出一个有立场但承认反方的判断。这就是 synoptic 的含义：不是"很多知识"，而是"知识之间的连接"。

2. The Synoptic Skill — What Makes Section E Different（综述技能）

Spec 9.3 opens with a sentence that defines the whole section: "Students must consider issues and debates from across all topics in order to develop a general knowledge of key issues and debates." The words from across all topics are the instruction.

❌ What a weak Section E answer does

  • Recalls one topic in depth and stops there
  • Describes a debate (AO1) but never assesses it
  • Uses only generic assertions ("science is good")
  • Ends with no judgement, or a one-sided one

✅ What a strong Section E answer does

  • Pulls evidence from three or more topics
  • Uses each example to advance an argument (AO3)
  • Weighs competing positions explicitly
  • Ends with a balanced, justified judgement

The AO1 : AO3 split — 8 marks vs 12 marks

Every Section E question is worth 20 marks split AO1 (8) + AO3 (12). Read that carefully: more than half the marks are for assessment, not knowledge.

Assessment objectiveWhat it rewardsMarks
AO1Knowledge & understandingAccurate, thorough, relevant knowledge — theories, studies, concepts, terminology.8
AO3AssessmentLogical chains of reasoning; weighing competing arguments; significance; a balanced judgement.12

Common trap: AO1 overload

The mark scheme states: "Knowledge & understanding is capped at maximum 8 marks." This means a candidate can write four perfect textbook pages of description and still cannot score above an 8/20 unless they develop a genuine assessment. Describe less, weigh more.

⚡ The Section E golden rule

For every piece of knowledge you introduce, immediately ask: "So what does this show about the debate?" If you cannot answer that in the same paragraph, the knowledge is doing no work for your AO3.

4. Deep Dive — Psychology as a Science（心理学作为一门科学）

Debate 9.3.5 · The issue of psychology as a science spec 9.3.5

This is the single most-tested debate in Section E — it appeared as WPS04 Q11 (20 marks). It asks whether psychology deserves the label "science", which forces you to define what a science is, then test psychology against that definition.

What counts as "science"? (AO1 foundations)

  • Empiricism（经验主义）: science studies physical evidence observable through the senses, rather than relying on opinion or introspection.
  • Objectivity（客观性）: science requires an unbiased, value-free analysis of data.
  • Falsification（可证伪性）: Popper argued that a scientific theory must be testable in a way that could disprove it — set out to disprove your hypothesis, not just confirm it.
  • Replication（可重复性）: standardised controls and laboratory experiments allow others to repeat a study and check reliability.
  • Universal laws（普遍规律）: science seeks a single general explanation for a phenomenon.

Testing psychology against those criteria

Criterion of sciencePsychology meets it……but falls short
Observable evidencePavlov (1927) measured salivation in dogs using a collection jar — a directly observable, measurable response.Some theories are introspective and lack empirical testing of observable phenomena (e.g. Freud's unconscious).
Objectivity / value-freeRaine et al. (1997) used PET scans — a value-free data-collection process.The interpretation of those scans is subjective, so psychology is not fully scientific.
FalsificationBandura applied falsification to SLT, revising it into Social Cognitive Theory when his research did not fully explain learning.Psychoanalysis is introspective — its concepts cannot be falsified, yet the therapy still works.
ReplicationMilgram (1963) used standardised procedures, letting Burger (2009) replicate them and retest the findings.Bartlett (1932) used loose controls, reducing the scientific nature of his memory research.
Universal lawsCognitive and biological approaches (nomothetic) seek universal laws, which is scientifically credible.Those laws ignore the uniqueness of individuals; the dopamine hypothesis reduces complex behaviour unrealistically.
How to use this in the essay: Do not list these like a checklist. Instead, take a criterion, apply it to two contrasting pieces of psychology, and reach a mini-conclusion — e.g. "On falsification, Bandura's revision of SLT suggests psychology can be scientific; but on the same criterion psychoanalysis fails, so the answer is 'partly'." That is AO3 in action.
中文理解：这道题的核心陷阱是"只答一面"。很多同学一上来就写"心理学当然科学，因为 Milgram 用了标准化程序"——这只能拿到很低的分数。考官想看到的是：你先定义科学的标准（可观察、客观、可证伪、可重复），然后逐条检验心理学，发现它"有的达标、有的不达标"。比如脑扫描够客观，但解读扫描还是主观的；学习理论能证伪，但精神分析不能。最后结论应该是"心理学是部分科学"或"科学程度因分支而异"，而不是简单的"是"或"不是"。

5. Deep Dive — Reductionism vs Holism（还原论与整体论）

Debate 9.3.3 · Reductionism versus holism spec 9.3.3

Reductionism explains complex behaviour by breaking it into smaller, simpler components (often biological or stimulus–response). Holism argues that behaviour can only be understood by studying the whole person in context.

🔬 Reductionist explanations

  • Depression: the monoamine hypothesis reduces depression to low serotonin/noradrenaline — a purely neurochemical account.
  • Aggression: MAOA gene and testosterone reduce a social behaviour to biology alone.
  • Schizophrenia: the dopamine hypothesis explains a whole disorder through one neurotransmitter.
  • Learning: classical conditioning reduces fear or attachment to stimulus–response bonds.

🌍 Holistic explanations

  • Depression: Beck's cognitive triad adds thoughts; the diathesis–stress model adds life events.
  • Schizophrenia: combining biology + family dysfunction + cognitive + social factors.
  • Aggression: social learning theory locates the cause in observation, imitation and culture.
  • Wellbeing: the biopsychosocial model, or the Māori te whare tapa whā view (mind, body, family, spirit).

Assessment — how to weigh the debate (AO3)

  • Reductionism is not a weakness by definition. Reducing behaviour to measurable variables is what makes psychology scientific and testable. The dopamine hypothesis became testable precisely because it was simplified.
  • But reductionism can mislead. The dopamine hypothesis excludes interactions between the individual and the environment, giving a scientific explanation that is unrealistic when studying the complexity of human nature.
  • Holism captures complexity but risks being untestable and vague — if everything matters, nothing can be isolated.
  • Interactionist middle ground: the diathesis–stress model (a genetic vulnerability triggered by environment) keeps the rigour of reduction while acknowledging holism.
中文理解：用生活化比喻——还原论像把一台电脑"拆成零件"去理解它（CPU、内存、电源）；整体论像坚持"只有让电脑跑起来、看它和网络、用户怎么互动，才能真正理解它"。拆零件的好处是看得清、测得准（科学性强）；坏处是拆完之后"电脑"这个整体就不见了。心理学的两难就在这儿：想科学，就得拆；想真实，就得看整体。

6. Deep Dive — Nature, Nurture & Development over Time（先天后天与发展）

6.1 Nature and nurture（先天与后天）

Debate 9.3.7 · The role of both nature and nurture spec 9.3.7

Do genes, the environment, or their interaction cause behaviour? Modern psychology answers almost always: interaction.

BehaviourNature evidenceNurture evidenceInteraction

6.2 How psychology has developed over time（心理学的发展）

Debate 9.3.8 · Understanding how psychology has developed over time spec 9.3.8

Psychology is not fixed: its methods, classifications and ideas have changed, and recognising that change is itself an evaluative point.

  • Method: from Wundt's introspection (1879) → behaviourism's objective observation → the cognitive revolution → today's brain imaging.
  • Classification: diagnostic systems have evolved (DSM-I to DSM-5; ICD-11), and once-listed conditions (e.g. homosexuality) have been removed — showing classification reflects social values as well as science.
  • Treatment: the prefrontal lobotomy of the 1930s–50s was replaced by drug therapy, then CBT — evidence that practice improves as knowledge does.
  • Aetiology: explanations of schizophrenia have shifted from "schizophrenogenic mother" to the dopamine hypothesis to combined biopsychosocial models.
Exam tip: "Development over time" is a gift for a conclusion. Even when a question is about a different debate, you can close by noting that the debate itself has shifted — e.g. "Early nature–nurture debates were polarised, but over time the field has moved toward interactionism."
中文理解："Nature vs nurture" 是入门级的老问题，但考试要的答案是升级版：不要停在"两者都重要"，而要给出交互机制——比如 Caspi 的研究发现，只有"MAOA 基因 + 童年受虐"同时出现时攻击性才升高。这就是"基因提供底稿，环境决定是否翻开"。

7. Deep Dive — Cultural, Gender & Socially Sensitive Issues（文化、性别与敏感性）

7.1 Cultural and gender issues（文化与性别议题）

Debate 9.3.6 · Cultural and gender issues spec 9.3.6

Ethnocentrism is judging other cultures by the standards of one's own; gender bias is treating one gender as the norm. Both threaten the claim that psychology studies "people" rather than "Western men".

  • Ethnocentric samples: Rosenhan (1973) was conducted in the USA in the 1970s — findings may not generalise; Ma et al. (2014) used only mainland Chinese undergraduates; Becker et al. (2002) studied Fijian girls but imported a Western "thin ideal".
  • Cultural bias in diagnosis: what is "statistically infrequent" or "violates moral standards" varies between cultures — the DSM-5 added a Cultural Formulation Interview in response.
  • Gender bias: anorexia research has historically used female samples (e.g. Reichel et al. 2014 included no males); Milgram's obedience samples were overwhelmingly male. Conclusions may not apply to the excluded group.

7.2 Socially sensitive research（社会敏感性研究）

Debate 9.3.11 · Issues relating to socially sensitive research spec 9.3.11

Research is socially sensitive when its findings could have consequences for a person, group or society — even when the research itself is methodologically sound.

  • Genetic explanations of aggression or mental illness risk being read as "born bad", fuelling stigma, discrimination or eugenic policies.
  • Biological accounts of depression can shift blame onto the individual ("it's just your brain chemistry") and downplay social causes such as poverty.
  • Labelling and diagnosis (Rosenhan) show how a label can change how others treat a person — a real social consequence of psychological findings.
  • Researchers' responsibility: findings should be reported tentatively and scientifically, with attention to how they may be interpreted or misused.
中文理解："社会敏感性"不是问研究"做得对不对"，而是问"结论传出去之后会不会伤人"。比如一篇研究发现"某个基因和暴力有关"，方法上可能很严谨，但如果被媒体简化成"暴力是天生的"，就可能变成对某些群体的歧视。所以心理学家有责任把话说得谨慎——这叫 scientific responsibility（科学责任）。

8. Deep Dive — Ethical & Practical Issues（伦理与实操问题）

8.1 Ethical issues in research（研究中的伦理议题）

Debate 9.3.1 · Ethical issues (animal and human) spec 9.3.1

Research with humans must meet the BPS Code of Ethics (2009) and, for practitioners, HCPC standards; research with animals is governed by the Scientific Procedures Act 1986.

PrincipleWhat it requiresClassic example

8.2 Practical issues in design and implementation（设计与实施中的实操问题）

Debate 9.3.2 · Practical issues spec 9.3.2

Even a perfect design can be impossible or impractical to run — and that shapes what psychology knows.

  • Time and cost: PET and fMRI scans are expensive and scarce, limiting sample size and replication.
  • Access to samples: truly random sampling is often impossible, so research relies on volunteers (with their own bias).
  • Attrition: longitudinal studies lose participants over time (e.g. Becker's prospective design).
  • Control vs realism: laboratory control raises internal validity but lowers ecological validity; field studies (Piliavin) gain realism but lose control.
  • Demand characteristics: participants may guess the aim and change their behaviour.
中文理解：伦理问题问"该不该这么做"，实操问题问"能不能这么做"。比如随机控制试验（RCT）是检验治疗效果的金标准，但你要把抑郁症患者随机分到"不接受治疗"的对照组，这在伦理上很难通过——于是只能用安慰剂对照。伦理和实操往往互相拉扯，这也是高分答案可以点出的张力。

9. Deep Dive — Social Control & Knowledge in Society（社会控制与知识应用）

9.1 The use of psychology in social control（社会控制）

Debate 9.3.9 · The use of psychology in social control spec 9.3.9

Psychological knowledge can be applied to shape and control behaviour — sometimes for care, sometimes as coercion.

  • Behaviour modification: token economies in schizophrenia care use operant conditioning to reinforce desirable behaviour — arguably therapeutic, arguably controlling.
  • Persuasion and propaganda: learning theory explains how attitudes can be conditioned; researchers such as Packard warned of "hidden persuaders".
  • Obedience to authority: Milgram showed how destructive obedience can be engineered — a warning about the power of situational control.
  • Assessment: where does therapy end and coercion begin? Token economies may be seen as ethical treatment or as taking away patients' autonomy.

9.2 The use of psychological knowledge in society（知识的社会应用）

Debate 9.3.10 · The use of psychological knowledge in society spec 9.3.10

The positive counterpart to social control: psychology can be applied to improve society.

  • Health literacy: understanding mental health literacy can reduce stigma and encourage help-seeking (Chapter 26).
  • Workplaces: O'Moore & Lynch (2007) linked friendly work environments to lower bullying — an applied route to a social problem.
  • Policy and education: evidence from eyewitness research, clinical trials and workplace studies informs real decisions.
  • Assessment: application can be powerful, but only as reliable as the underlying research — weak or culture-bound findings can misguide policy.
中文理解：这两个议题是一枚硬币的两面。社会控制偏"操控"——用心理学让人听话、守规矩；知识应用偏"帮助"——用心理学让社会更好。同一项技术（比如行为矫正）可以被看作治疗，也可以被看作控制，取决于谁在用、用来干什么。Section E 里，能同时戴这两副眼镜看同一件事，就是高分的标志。

10. The 20-Mark Toolkit — Planning a Section E Essay（20 分论文工具箱）

A 20-mark synoptic essay is not improvised. Use the same four-move structure every time.

🧩 The four-move structure

  1. Define the terms and set the frame (~3–4 marks AO1). Define the debate itself, then state how you will test it. E.g. "To assess whether psychology is a science, I first need criteria: falsifiability, objectivity, replication."
  2. Build the "for" case (AO1 + AO3). Bring evidence from at least two topics that supports one side. Always pair the evidence with an inference.
  3. Build the "against" case (AO1 + AO3). Bring contrasting evidence — ideally from different topics — and explain why it complicates the picture.
  4. Reach a balanced judgement (AO3). Weigh the two cases. Do not sit on the fence: give a reasoned position, qualified by what limits it.

The AO3 moves that earn the top level

  • Chains of reasoning: "Because X, therefore Y, which suggests Z" — never a bare assertion.
  • Competing arguments: explicitly acknowledge the opposing view rather than ignoring it.
  • Significance: explain why the point matters to the debate, not just that it exists.
  • Synoptic range: name the topic each piece of evidence comes from (a marker of genuine breadth).
  • Balanced judgement: the Level 4 (17–20) descriptor requires "a full understanding and awareness of the significance of competing arguments/factors leading to a balanced judgement".
LevelMarksWhat the examiner wants
11–4Isolated knowledge; generic assertions; limited attempt to address the question.
25–8Mostly accurate knowledge; some development; a generic or superficial assessment.
39–12Coherent chains of reasoning; considers a range of factors; a judgement, but imbalanced.
413–16Logical assessment; competing arguments understood; judgement still somewhat imbalanced.
517–20Well-developed logical assessment; full awareness of the significance of competing arguments; a balanced judgement.

Two killers of a Section E essay

  • Description without assessment — capped at 8 marks however long you write.
  • Single-topic answers — if all your evidence comes from one chapter, the answer is not synoptic and cannot reach the top levels.

11. Worked Example — WPS04 Q11 "Assess whether psychology can be considered a science"（真题范文）

📝 The question (WPS04/01, Section E, Q11 — 20 marks)

"Assess whether psychology can be considered a science." (20)

AO1 (8 marks) · AO3 (12 marks)

Step 1 — Decode the command word

"Assess" = weigh up both sides and reach a judgement. The question is not "describe the scientific method" — it is "make a case, then judge it".

Step 2 — Plan the synoptic range

TopicEvidence to useServes which side?
Learning (D)Pavlov (1927) salivation in dogs — measurable, observable✅ Science
Social (A)Milgram (1963) standardised procedure → replicated by Burger (2009)✅ Science
Biological (C)Raine et al. (1997) PET scans — value-free data collection✅ Science (with a limit)
Cognitive (B)Bartlett (1932) loose controls; Peterson & Peterson (1959) artificial trigrams❌ Not fully scientific
Clinical / approachesQualitative data (natural observations); psychoanalysis is unfalsifiable❌ Not fully scientific

Step 3 — Gold-standard model answer (Level 5 standard)

Introduction — set the frame

Whether psychology can be considered a science depends on what we mean by "science". A science is generally taken to require the empirical study of observable evidence, objectivity through value-free analysis, the possibility of falsification, and replication via standardised controls. Judged against these criteria, psychology is partly scientific — some of its methods meet the standard while others fall short.

For — psychology meets scientific criteria

Learning theory demonstrates scientific principles: Pavlov (1927) measured dogs' salivation using a collection jar, producing directly observable, quantitative data — behaviour that could be recorded and repeated. Similarly, Milgram (1963) used a standardised procedure in his obedience studies, which allowed both himself and later researchers such as Burger (2009) to replicate the findings and retest their reliability. Replication is central to science, and here psychology delivers it. In the biological approach, Raine et al. (1997) used PET scans, a value-free data-collection process, to compare murderers pleading NGRI with controls. These examples suggest psychology can adopt the empirical, standardised methods of the natural sciences.

Against — psychology falls short of scientific criteria

However, the same evidence reveals limits. Although Raine's scans were collected objectively, their interpretation is subjective — deciding what a pattern of activity "means" involves judgement, so psychology is not fully scientific. Some psychological theories are introspective and lack empirical testing of observable phenomena; psychoanalysis, for instance, is introspective and its concepts cannot be falsified, yet it is still used therapeutically — showing that not all psychology even aims for scientific status. Standardised methods can also buy rigour at the cost of realism: Peterson and Peterson (1959) tested short-term memory using nonsense trigrams that do not represent a realistic use of memory, creating a tension between science and real behaviour. Bartlett (1932), conversely, avoided strict controls in his memory research, reducing its scientific nature — though it may be a more valid representation of real-life memory. Finally, reductionism, such as the dopamine hypothesis, excludes interactions between the individual and the environment, giving a scientific but unrealistic account of human complexity, and nomothetic approaches seek universal laws that, however scientific, ignore the uniqueness of individuals.

Judgement — a balanced conclusion

On balance, psychology can be considered partially scientific, and its scientific status varies by approach. Where it uses observable measures, standardised procedures and replication — as in behaviourist and some biological research — it meets the criteria of science. Where it relies on introspection, unfalsifiable concepts or artificial tasks, it does not. Crucially, the field itself has developed over time: Bandura applied falsification to SLT and revised it into Social Cognitive Theory when his research did not fully explain learning, making the theory more scientific. This suggests psychology is not fixed — it is becoming more scientific as its methods and theories mature, even if it cannot yet be described as a science in the same sense as physics.

Why this scores Level 5

  • AO1Accurate knowledge of criteria, studies and terminology across four topics.
  • AO3Each example is used to advance an argument, not merely described.
  • SYNEvidence is drawn from Learning, Social, Biological, Cognitive and the approaches debate.
  • AO3A balanced judgement that weighs both sides and gives a reasoned position.
中文理解：注意范文的结构是"定义标准 → 正方 2–3 个主题 → 反方 2–3 个主题 → 判断"。每一段都在做同一件事：先给证据，再解释这条证据对辩论意味着什么。结尾没有含糊地说"两边都有道理"，而是给出了明确立场——"部分科学，且科学程度因分支而异，并随时间提高"，同时点出"心理学还在进化"。这就是考官要的 balanced judgement。

13. Key Terms（关键术语表）

TermDefinition
SynopticDrawing on knowledge from across the whole qualification, not one topic.
Issue / debateA recurring, contested question in psychology (e.g. is it a science?).
ReductionismExplaining complex behaviour by breaking it into smaller, simpler components.
HolismExplaining behaviour by studying the whole person in context.
FalsificationPopper's principle that a scientific theory must be testable in a way that could disprove it.
ReplicationRepeating a study with standardised procedures to check reliability.
ObjectivityValue-free, unbiased data collection and analysis.
NomotheticSeeking universal laws that apply to all people.
IdiographicStudying the unique individual rather than universal laws.
EthnocentrismJudging other cultures by the standards of one's own.
Gender biasTreating one gender as the norm, so findings may not apply to the other.
Diathesis–stressModel where a genetic vulnerability is triggered by environmental stress.
Social controlUsing psychological knowledge to shape or direct people's behaviour.
Socially sensitive researchResearch whose findings could have negative consequences for a group or society.
Balanced judgementA reasoned, qualified conclusion that weighs competing arguments — required for Level 5.
▶ Exam Key Points — Whole Chapter AO1 · AO3 · Synoptic
  1. Section E = one 20-mark synoptic essay on issues and debates; AO1 (8) + AO3 (12).
  2. Knowledge is capped at 8 marks — assessment, not description, earns the top levels.
  3. Spec 9.3 lists eleven debates; draw evidence from paths A–H, never a single chapter.
  4. Science (9.3.5) is the most-tested; use criteria (falsifiability, objectivity, replication) to test psychology.
  5. Reductionism vs holism, nature vs nurture and ways of explaining are causal debates — favour interactionist conclusions.
  6. Cultural/gender bias and socially sensitive research are about who research applies to and who it may harm.
  7. Social control vs knowledge in society are two sides of psychology's impact on people.
  8. Structure: define → for → against → balanced judgement; every example must carry an inference (AO3).
