4.2 METHODS
Observation · Content analysis · Case study · Chi-squared · Animal research · 学习理论研究方法

Topic D · Learning theories and development Methods Spec 4.2.1–4.2.8

What this chapter is really asking

  1. An observation has no IV. Nothing is manipulated; all data comes from watching and recording behaviour. That single fact drives every strength and weakness.
  2. Six type words, three pairs. Naturalistic or structured, participant or non-participant, overt or covert. Any real observation is a combination of one word from each pair.
  3. Two indirect methods. Content analysis watches things people made (books, adverts); Freud's case study watched what clients said on the couch.
  4. Chi-squared has three entry conditions: a test of difference or association, nominal data, independent measures.
  5. Animal research needs the law: the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act (1986), the three Rs and the three licences.

1. Getting Started — Plan an Observation

Scenario from the textbook: observe different types of communication in an office. Some areas are open plan; other areas have desks divided by screens. The prediction: an open plan space encourages verbal communication, while cubicles promote electronic communication.

The five planned steps (from the textbook)

  1. Decide what behaviour you will observe and who you will observe.
  2. Decide where you will observe the behaviour.
  3. Make a table to record the observed behaviour.
  4. Decide whether you will record all behaviour in a timeframe or tally specific behaviours as they occur.
  5. Analyse your findings.
中文理解：观察和实验的分界线
这一章所有方法共用一个前提：观察法不操纵 IV。实验里研究者动手改变什么条件再测结果；观察里研究者只站在旁边看和记。考试里问「为什么用观察而不用实验」，第一句就写这一点：研究的问题不适合操纵变量（比如想知道办公室里真实的沟通方式），所以只能记录自然发生的行为。课本的五步清单就是 4.4 实操大题的答题骨架，先把顺序背熟。

3. Gathering Qualitative and Quantitative Data

Observation as a research method means observing is the main way of obtaining data and there is no independent variable to manipulate. All data is gathered by observing, listening and recording behaviour.

Quantitative data · numbers

Data that can be counted or expressed numerically. Example from the textbook: counting the number of times a child you observe picks up a gender-stereotyped toy（性别刻板玩具）.

Qualitative data · words

Data that cannot be expressed numerically; it is concerned with words, texts, ideas and themes. Example from the textbook: recording what parents say to their child and any non-verbal communication they use.

Data collection tools

  • Tallying（计数记录）: observers write down when and how many times certain behaviours occurred.
  • Notes taken during the session, reviewed later to find behavioural patterns.
  • Audio or video recordings.

The tally chart

Table 23.1 from the textbook: a researcher observes whether men and women prefer different sized cars. Each time they see a person in a category, they tally in the matching cell.

4. Types of Observation — Three Pairs of Choices

Pair 1 · Naturalistic vs structured

naturalistic observation（自然观察） — watching and recording behaviour in a natural environment where the observed behaviour would normally occur.
  • Naturalistic: the situation has not been created by the researcher, so it gives a real insight into behaviour. Higher ecological validity because participants act naturally in a familiar setting. But it poses ethical issues of consent and privacy if participants do not know they are watched, has limited control over extraneous variables, and is unlikely to be repeated exactly.
  • Structured（结构观察）: variables are manipulated to make sure the behaviour is displayed. Staged observations, normally in an environment the researcher controls such as a laboratory, sometimes through a one-way screen.

Worked example: Ainsworth's Strange Situation

Mary Ainsworth et al. (1978) wanted to investigate parent–child attachment in a controlled way, but the sequence of events (mother leaves, stranger enters, reunion) would rarely occur naturally. So she set up the Strange Situation Procedure: a structured observation in a controlled environment.

Pair 2 · Participant vs non-participant

non-participant observation（非参与观察） — the researcher observes behaviour of others but does not form part of the group they study. participant observation（参与观察） — the researcher takes an active role in the situation being observed.
  • Non-participant: observing from a distance. If participants notice, they may alter their behaviour. Because the observer is detached they may not understand why a behaviour happens, or may interpret it subjectively: observer bias（观察者偏差）. The gain: researchers can concentrate on recording and collect data more objectively.
  • Participant: joining the group. No stranger effect on how participants behave, and it can reveal data other methods would miss. But it is hard to record notes while taking part, and the researcher may lose objectivity.

Pair 3 · Overt vs covert

overt observation（公开观察） — those being observed are aware of the presence of an observer. covert observation（隐蔽观察） — the participants do not know they are being observed.
  • Overt: informed consent can be obtained and participants keep the right to withdraw. But knowing they are watched may change behaviour.
  • Covert: ethically problematic because participants are unaware. But participants are unlikely to change their behaviour, so the observation is more valid.

Any observation is a combination

The textbook's two examples: a naturalistic, participant, overt observation, such as an anthropologist joining a tribal group to learn its customs; or a structured, non-participant, covert observation, such as secretly watching behaviour behind a one-way mirror in a laboratory.

Exam tip (from the textbook): be ready not only to outline each type but to give examples. Explicitly state which type you are describing or evaluating. Ethical and methodological issues that apply to one type may not apply to another: participant observations affect the behaviour of those observed, but that may not be true of covert non-participant observations.

6. Content Analysis

Content analysis may not involve observing participants' behaviour directly, but instead observing the incidence of other things: certain words, images or concepts within material such as advertisements, books, films and newspapers. Example from the textbook: a researcher uses content analysis to observe instances of gender stereotyping in children's books. Content analysis is therefore a form of indirect observation（间接观察）.

How to conduct one

  • Categories agreed in advance: for gender stereotyping, examine instances of stereotypical and non-stereotypical behaviour by males and females.
  • Tally the number of times each category occurs in the selected material.
  • Qualitative analysis can also examine meanings and relationships of words, concepts and pictures, then make inferences about the messages within them.
  • The content must be coded into clear and manageable categories so appropriate conclusions can be drawn.

Evaluation

Strength 1 · Unobtrusive（不打扰）

Data is collected from existing sources rather than live participants, so there are rarely ethical issues; researchers should still respect confidentiality.

Strength 2 · Reliable and useful over time

Reliability can easily be assessed because the analysis can be replicated using the same categories. It offers fresh interpretation of existing data and is very useful for historical material and documenting trends over time.

Limitation 1 · Subjective（主观）

It relies on the personal interpretation of the researcher. Internal validity is also at risk if the categories do not represent what they intend to measure.

Limitation 2 · Descriptive only

It describes what is there but not why: it may not reveal the underlying motives for the observed pattern. It is limited by the availability of material; media trends may not reflect reality because dramatic events receive more coverage; and findings may be culture-biased if sources or coders come from one culture.

7. Freud's Use of the Case Study

A case study（个案研究） is a detailed investigation into one individual or a small group, typically used when someone is unique or rare, or has experienced something that could not ethically or practically be studied in a laboratory. Many methods can be combined inside one case study, such as observations, interviews and experiments, and their outcomes compared for trends.

What Freud actually did

  • He gathered in-depth information from clients using free association（自由联想）, dream analysis（梦的分析） and observations of clients' behaviour to uncover unconscious wishes and desires.
  • Clients lay on a couch facing away from him to encourage them to talk without censorship.
  • He looked for Freudian slips（弗洛伊德式口误）: when a client says something they did not mean, because the unconscious reveals itself.
  • He analysed dreams and looked for transference and counter-transference（移情与反移情）.
  • He analysed the qualitative data and gave his interpretation of the unconscious to the client. His case studies informed his theories and were also used as a therapy.

Evaluation

Strength 1 · Sometimes the only way

Case studies gather information that cannot ethically be achieved by other methods: Freud could not have caused hysteria in a client to research it. Clients had to present themselves with the symptoms.

Strength 2 · Deep and natural

High ecological validity: data came directly from the individual in a natural setting. He gathered rich detailed information through free association and dream analysis, which he used both to treat the patient and develop his theories.

Limitation 1 · Unrepresentative（难以推广）

He studied unique individuals, typically middle-class Viennese women, so it is difficult to generalise the information to anyone else.

Limitation 2 · Subjective and unverifiable

Different analysts may find different meanings in the same free association or dream symbol, so the method is subjective and hard to build a general theory from. The unconscious cannot be directly observed, so the information could not be empirically verified（实证检验）.

中文理解：弗洛伊德方法的死穴在哪
四条评价都围着两个词转：深和主观。他躺在沙发上的做法换来了细致的信息（自然情境、细节丰富、边研究边治疗）；主观也是这套流程带来的（不同分析师解出不同含义，潜意识又无法实证）。考试写评价时把这条因果链点出来：方法的优点和缺点出自同一套做法，这就是高级 AO3。

8. The Chi-Squared Test（卡方检验）

The chi-squared test is a test of association or difference conducted on nominal data（称名数据）, when results are gathered in groups or categories and recorded as frequencies.

Three conditions for choosing chi-squared

  1. The hypothesis predicts a difference or an association (for example boys and girls differ in the toys they choose).
  2. The level of measurement is nominal (categories such as stereotypical or non-stereotypical toys).
  3. The design uses independent measures（独立测量设计）: at least two different groups, for example boys and girls.
Exam tip (from the textbook): a clue that chi-squared is needed is that frequencies are totalled, as in observational research or content analysis. It is not used when participants' individual scores are known. Do not convert numbers into averages or percentages; use the total number.

Worked example: toy choice at nursery

A researcher asks whether four-year-old girls and boys play with gender-stereotypical toys. The data goes into a contingency table（列联表） where each cell shows a frequency:

Stereotypical toyNon-stereotypical toyRow total
Girls8 (Cell A)12 (Cell B)20
Boys17 (Cell C)3 (Cell D)20
Column total251540 (overall)

9. Animal Research — Arguments in Favour

Learning theorists such as Pavlov and Skinner built their methods on laboratory experimentation with non-human (animal) participants, manipulating an IV to see its effect on behaviour. The textbook's arguments in favour:

1 · Practical breeding and speed

Rats are frequently used because of their breeding speed: a rat's gestation period is typically 22 days, so large samples can be bred quickly, and effects across generations (for example aggression) show without long waits.

2 · High control

Animals can be caged in ways humans cannot, and variables isolated in ways impossible with people: placing them in a Skinner box and dispensing food on a lever press.

3 · Fewer ethical limits than humans

Unnecessary pain must be avoided, but pain and distress are permitted, which is not the case for human participants; Skinner gave electric shocks to animals in his box. Research is nonetheless heavily regulated: legislation governs housing and treatment, and laboratories undergo regular welfare inspections.

4 · Real benefits and evolutionary continuity

Animal research has provided significant insight into drug treatments, transplants, surgical techniques and cloning, and into the brain and nervous system; conditioning techniques from it are used in therapy. Humans share common ancestry with other animals, especially primates, with similar basic nervous systems, so we can generalise to some extent. The utilitarian argument（功利主义论证）: the suffering of a small number of animals is justified because it helps a significant number of people; Gray (1991) argues animal research is justified when it furthers this cause.

10. Animal Research — Arguments Against

1 · Lacks credibility and validity

Lab-based animal research produces behaviour different from natural behaviour, so ecological validity is low.

2 · Humans and animals differ too much

Differences in evolution and genetic make-up; we differ cognitively and emotionally, and human behaviour is subject to far more variables such as culture, social norms and language. Physiologically, humans have a much larger cortex proportionally than any other animal. A newborn mouse in development and hormonal influence is equivalent to a seven-week-old foetus: can comparisons really be made?

3 · Anthropomorphism and the continuity contradiction

Generalising from animals to humans risks anthropomorphism（拟人化）: mistakenly attributing human qualities to animals. Others point to the inconsistency in the continuity argument: if animals are so similar to humans, why are they not afforded the same ethical considerations?

4 · Speciesism（物种歧视）

The benefits of research are not known until after the study, so the animal may suffer for limited effect. Singer (1975) viewed giving human suffering priority over animal suffering as discrimination: speciesism. On this view animals have the same rights as humans and no amount of regulation can justify animal research.

中文理解：争议的根在「连续性」这个双刃剑
支持方的核心论据是连续性：人和动物共享演化祖先和神经系统，所以结果能推广。反方用同一把刀反击：既然这么相似，凭什么不给它们同样的伦理保护？再往前一步就是 Singer 的物种歧视。考试写「评价动物研究」时，把这条连续性正反两用的思路写出来，比各堆三条理由更能拿高分。

11. Animal Ethics — The Law and the Three Rs

The legislation

  • The Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act (1986) covers all animal research conducted in the UK. It relates to any scientific procedure that may cause pain, suffering, distress or lasting harm to a 'protected' animal. Protected animals comprise all non-human vertebrates and a single invertebrate species (the octopus).
  • Psychologists are also directed to follow the Animal Welfare Act (2006), which sets general duties of care towards animals.
  • In Europe, national regulations are governed by Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals for scientific research.

The BPS position and the three Rs

The British Psychological Society (BPS) states that permission for animal procedures will not be granted unless the researcher can justify the costs to the animals in relation to the likely benefits. Curiosity alone is not enough. When permission is requested, the researcher must demonstrate consideration of the three Rs (Russell and Burch, 1959) to minimise pain and distress:

Three RsMeaning
Replace（替代）Use non-sentient alternatives whenever possible (animals showing no signs of intelligence or self-awareness)
Reduce（减少）Use fewer animals
Refine（优化）Refine procedures to minimise suffering

The three licences

LicenceWhat it regulates
Project licence（项目许可）Each project gets a new licence specifying the species and number of animals; granted only when benefits outweigh costs to the animals
Personal licence（个人许可）For each researcher, given only after training proving competence to perform the procedures
Place licence（场所许可）Regulates the conditions in which animals are kept, such as housing; these places are regularly inspected

Researchers must minimise pain, suffering or distress within the design; adverse effects must be recognised and assessed with immediate action when necessary, and the licensed researcher is responsible for this happening.

中文理解：三 R 加三证是必背清单
课本 checkpoint 原题就问「说出 three Rs 和 three Ps」：Replace、Reduce、Refine 加 Project、Personal、Place 三张许可。记忆法：三 R 管的是怎么对待动物（换掉、减量、减痛），三证管的是谁批准谁来做在哪做（项目、个人、场所）。立法背景记一个锚点就够：1986 年法案 + 八爪鱼是唯一受保护的无脊椎动物，这个细节考过选择题。

13. Exam Lab

Textbook exam practice AO1 + AO2 + AO3 8-mark evaluation

Question 1 — Niro's glass walkway (from the textbook)

Niro was investigating heights as a phobia. At the top of a tall building with a glass walkway, visitors could choose the glass path or an avoiding path. Niro observed how many people chose each path.

✍️ 1 — Strength and weakness4 marks

Explain one strength and one weakness of Niro using an observation research method in this study. (4)

Strength (ecological validity): the observation took place in a natural setting where the behaviour would normally occur, so visitors behaved as they really would when choosing a path, and the findings reflect genuine avoidance of heights rather than artificial laboratory behaviour. Weakness (lack of control): extraneous variables such as the weather, how busy the building was, or individual differences in bravery could not be controlled, so it is hard to be sure that path choice was caused by fear of heights alone, and the observation could not be repeated exactly.

中文方法框：4 分观察评价题怎么拆
一强一弱各两分：一分点出优缺点名词，一分挂回 Niro 的场景。优点选自然情境带来的生态效度，缺点选无法控制额外变量。注意别把优点说成「因为他是观察所以真实」，要写玻璃走道是真实环境、游客不知道被观察才落分。弱点同理：天气、人流、胆量都管不住。

Question 2 — Angelo's nursery observation (from the textbook)

Angelo observed children aged two to four in a local nursery. Each child was observed for five minutes and the number of times they played with each toy was tallied. Boys: construction-type toys 18, doll-type toys 32. Girls: construction-type 34, doll-type 12. Angelo used a chi-squared test and found a calculated value of 13.87.

✍️ 2a — Justify the test2 marks

Justify the use of a chi-squared test for this data. (2)

The data is nominal: frequencies of boys and girls playing with each category of toy, counted as tallies in categories rather than individual scores. The design uses independent measures (each child is either a boy or a girl, so the groups are unrelated) and the hypothesis predicts a difference between the groups, so chi-squared is the appropriate test of difference on nominal frequency data.

中文方法框：justify 卡方就说三条件
两分题的答案就是 §8 的三条入场条件，挑两条写满即可：称名数据（频次计数）、独立测量（男女两组互不重叠）、检验差异或关联。写成「因为数据是频次所以选卡方」只算一条，把独立组和检验类型也点上才稳。

✍️ 2b — Is it significant?2 marks

Angelo found a calculated value of 13.87. Explain whether this is significant at p ≤ 0.05, two-tailed, df = 1. (2)

With df = 1 the critical value at p ≤ 0.05 for a two-tailed test is 3.84. The observed value of 13.87 is greater than 3.84, so the result is significant: there is less than a five per cent probability that the difference in toy choice between boys and girls is due to chance, so the null hypothesis can be rejected.

✍️ Question 3 — Time sampling3 marks

Evuska observed whether drivers aged 25 or below, or 50 and above, were more likely to use their mobile phone while driving, using time sampling. Describe how Evuska may have carried out the observation using time sampling. (3)

Evuska would divide the observation period into fixed time intervals, for example recording every 30 seconds. At each interval she would record everything observed: whether each driver was in the younger or older age category and whether they were using a mobile phone at that moment. She would continue through the set intervals for the whole observation period and tally the frequencies for each group, so the data could later be compared between the two age categories.

中文方法框：描述时间抽样三步走
三分题就三句话：定间隔（每 30 秒或每 1 分钟）→ 窗口内全记（年龄段 + 是否用手机）→ 全程计数汇总。别写成事件抽样（那会写成分 behaviors 出现就记），也别忘了最后一句把数据落到两组对比上。

✍️ Question 4 — The 8-mark evaluation8 marks

Evaluate content analysis as a research method. (8)

Frame your answer: two strengths with explanation → two weaknesses with explanation → a brief conclusion.

Strength 1 (ethics and access): content analysis is unobtrusive because data comes from existing sources such as books, adverts or newspapers rather than live participants, so there are rarely ethical issues and material that could never be studied with people (for example historical documents) becomes available. Strength 2 (reliability): because the categories are decided in advance, another researcher can replicate the analysis using the same categories, so reliability can easily be assessed, and trends over time can be documented. Weakness 1 (subjectivity): the analysis relies on the personal interpretation of the researcher, and if the categories do not represent what they intend to measure the data lacks internal validity. Weakness 2 (descriptive only): it describes what is there but not why, so underlying motives stay hidden; it is also limited by the availability of material, and media trends may not reflect reality because dramatic events receive more coverage. Findings may be culture-biased if sources or researchers come from one culture. Overall, content analysis is a valuable indirect observation where live research is impossible, but conclusions should be checked across coders and cultures before being treated as firm.

14. Key Terms

naturalistic observation 自然观察
Watching and recording behaviour in a natural environment where the behaviour would normally occur.
structured observation 结构观察
Watching and recording behaviour in an artificial environment where a situation is manipulated to encourage a behaviour.
participant observation 参与观察
The researcher takes an active role in the situation being observed.
non-participant observation 非参与观察
The researcher observes behaviour of others but does not form part of the group they study.
overt observation 公开观察
Those being observed are aware of the presence of an observer.
covert observation 隐蔽观察
The participants do not know they are being observed.
observer bias 观察者偏差
A detached observer misinterprets behaviour subjectively because they do not fully understand why it is displayed.
operational definitions of behavioural categories 行为类目的操作性定义
Clear, unambiguous criteria for each observed category; observers need training in them.
time sampling 时间抽样
Observing at fixed time intervals and recording everything observed in each window.
event sampling 事件抽样
Recording a predetermined behaviour every time it happens.
content analysis 内容分析
An indirect observation of the incidence of words, images or concepts in material such as adverts, books and films.
case study 个案研究
A detailed investigation into one individual or a small group, combining several research methods.
Freudian slip 弗洛伊德式口误
When a client says something they did not mean; the unconscious revealing itself.
independent measures 独立测量设计
A design where different groups of participants are used in each condition of the study.
utilitarian argument 功利主义论证
In animal ethics, the view that a small number of animals suffering is justified because the research helps a significant number of people.
anthropomorphism 拟人化
Mistakenly attributing human qualities to non-human animals.
speciesism 物种歧视
Singer's (1975) term for prioritising human suffering over animal suffering.
Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act (1986)
UK legislation covering all animal research; protects all non-human vertebrates and the octopus.
three Rs (Russell and Burch, 1959)
Replace animals with non-sentient alternatives, reduce the number used, refine procedures to minimise suffering.
inter-observer reliability 观察者间信度
More than one observer conducts the observation and the rate of agreement is calculated and published with the data.
