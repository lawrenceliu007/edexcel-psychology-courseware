What this workbook covers 本册讲什么

This chapter names the three types of conformity (Kelman, 1958), the two explanations (Deutsch & Gerrard, 1955), conformity to social roles (Zimbardo), and one theory that covers the whole field: social impact theory (Latané, 1981).

§5 · Explanations 1 · informational social influenceANSWERS → p.19

Deutsch and Harold (1955) proposed two forms of social influence which explain conformity: social influence and social influence.

Informational social influence（ISI 信息性社会影响）
  • Happens where we are of how to act or think, so we look to to guide our actions.
  • We conform because we believe the majority is the way to behave — and we accept it.
  • Explains conformity in situations.
  • Involves a judgement based on the information available.
  • Leads to — we genuinely believe the majority view ( acceptance).
The textbook example

In a high-end restaurant, a diner looks at to see which to use for their starter course, because they do not know which fork is correct.

中文理解：一句话抓住 ISI
“我不知道，所以他们大概知道。”
触发条件：不确定（ambiguous）。过程：理性判断（rational）。结果：真的信 → internalisation（私下接受）。
关键词背下来：ambiguous · rational · information · correct · internalisation。

§6 · Explanations 2 · normative social influenceANSWERS → p.19
Normative social influence（NSI 规范性社会影响）
  • We conform because we have a need to be and to into a group — or to avoid .
  • pressure is a common form, particularly in when we want to feel a sense of belonging.
  • It can result in the types known as and — public acceptance without genuine belief.
The textbook example

In a friendship group, many members start listening to a new musician. all members of the group like this music — but they buy tickets to see the musician play live.

The evidence: Asch (1956)

Asch demonstrated NSI in a series of experiments where participants sometimes selected an answer that was incorrect because of pressure to conform to the group judgement. The correct answer was clear — so they could not be conforming for information. (Full detail in 1.1.5.)

The catch — one criticism built into the evidence

These studies support ISI and NSI as explanations. However, they were conducted in controlled conditions. While they demonstrate the process of majority influence, they tell us little about social influence in situations, such as peer pressure in a school or workplace.

Evidence table 用法
评价题里 ISI/NSI 的支持证据三选二就够：Jenness（模糊任务·豆子）、Sherif（模糊任务·光点）、Asch（明确任务·线段）。
注意结构：两个模糊任务支持 ISI，一个明确任务支持 NSI —— 任务性质决定支持哪条解释，这个对应关系本身就是分析点。

p.16 — Q1a model (2 marks)

Fezile is showing compliance: she publicly goes along with her teacher's request to appear helpful, but she does not privately agree with it — she would rather be canoeing. Because compliance is only public acceptance, her helpful behaviour will last only while the teacher's pressure exists.

p.16 — Q1b model (2 marks)

Fezile is showing internalisation: she has genuinely accepted the value of training the children, so it has become her own belief — private acceptance. This is why she gives up canoeing with her friends even though no one is forcing her: the change is real and self-sustaining.

p.16 — Q2 model (2 marks)

Kasim conforms because of normative social influence: he changes his behaviour to match the majority of his friendship group even though he privately disagrees — he actually asked them to stop. His need to be accepted and to avoid rejection is stronger than his own opinion, so he complies publicly (compliance).

p.16 — Q3 model (3 marks)

① Informational social influence operates in situations where we are unsure how to act or think (ambiguous situations). ② We conform to the majority because we believe it is the correct way to behave, making a rational judgement based on the information the group provides. ③ Because we genuinely accept the majority view (private acceptance), it produces internalisation — the most permanent type of conformity. Sherif's (1935) autokinetic study is supporting evidence.

p.16 — Q4 model (4 marks)

① Latané (1981) proposed social impact theory: we are greatly influenced by others' actions — persuaded, inhibited, threatened and supported. ② The source is the influencer (in conformity, the majority) and the target is the person feeling the pressure (the individual). ③ Three social forces determine the impact: strength (status, legitimacy, authority of the source), immediacy (proximity between source and target) and number (how many sources there are — though the effect levels off, like light bulbs in a room). ④ The effect is multiplicative: impact is a function of all three combined — I = f(S·I·N) — so a source weak on one force has less total impact.

p.17 — Q5 model (8 marks)

¶1 Describe. Latané (1981) proposed that we are greatly influenced by the actions of others — we can be persuaded, inhibited, threatened and supported. He distinguished the source of influence (in conformity, the majority) from the target (the individual feeling real or imagined pressure).

¶2 Describe. Three social forces determine the impact: strength (the perceived status or authority of the source), immediacy (how close the source is) and number (how many sources — but with diminishing returns, like light bulbs: the first bulb adds the most light). The forces combine multiplicatively: I = f(S·I·N).

¶3 Evaluate — support. The theory is supported by field evidence: Berkowitz, Bickman and Milgram (1969) placed 1–15 confederates on a street craning their necks upward, and found more confederates produced more copycat behaviour — but the effect levelled off, exactly as the force of number predicts. Asch (1956) similarly found conformity rose to a majority of three but no further, and fell when a single dissenter appeared.

¶4 Evaluate — limitation and conclusion. However, SIT is reductionist: it views individuals as passive receivers of influence, ignoring what the target brings to the situation. Everyone differs — some are more independent and resistant — so the theory oversimplifies social interaction and cannot predict who will conform. Overall, SIT is a useful framework for comparing situations, but needs supplementing with individual-difference factors to explain actual behaviour.
