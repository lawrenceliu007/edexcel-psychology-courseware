6.1.5 FACTORS INFLUENCING JURY DECISION-MAKING
影响陪审团决策的因素

Criminological Psychology · characteristics of the defendant（被告特征）· pre-trial publicity（审前报道）· expert testimony（专家证词）
🧠 Core idea: a verdict should rest on evidence — but other things get in 🔑 Real juries cannot be studied, so nearly all evidence is mock juries 📖 Textbook: Chapter 10

1. Getting Started

Twelve ordinary people are chosen at random, told to look only at the evidence, and asked to decide whether someone goes to prison. It sounds like the fairest system anyone could design. The question this chapter asks is simple and uncomfortable: does the verdict actually depend only on the evidence?

TRY THIS The same case, five different verdicts

Imagine a robbery trial. The evidence is identical in every version below — same witness, same facts, same lawyer. Researchers changed one thing in each version and measured the verdict. Tick the ones you think would actually change the outcome.

先记住这一句话：陪审团制度的设计目标是「只看证据」。心理学研究的问题是——人做不到只看证据。这一章的三个因素，都是「不该影响判决、但确实影响了判决」的东西。

2. What a Jury Actually Does

Before we can ask what biases a jury, we need to know what a jury is meant to do. If someone is accused of a serious offence they receive a trial by jury.

FeatureWhat happens
WhoTypically 12 jurors, selected at random from an electoral roll or similar list of local people.
The rulesJurors must not discuss the case with anyone outside the jury, and must avoid media coverage of the case. This protects them from influences other than the evidence.
The judgeThe judge instructs the jury on the law, but cannot decide guilt and cannot tell the jury what to decide. The judge relies entirely on the jury.
In courtJurors hear evidence from prosecution and defence lawyers, witness testimony, the defendant's statement — and sometimes expert testimony.
DeliberationThe jury leaves the room to review the evidence and discuss it. Deliberation is completely confidential: nobody else may enter and it cannot be recorded. A foreperson gives the verdict back to the judge.

Why this matters for psychology

Because the jury's only job is to weigh evidence, anything that shifts that weighing without being evidence is a problem. Psychologists have found such influences at several points in the process: before the trial even starts, during the trial, and in how evidence is explained.

The research problem: we cannot watch real juries

If you wanted to study bias in real juries, you would need to sit in the deliberation room. That is not permitted — deliberations are confidential and cannot be recorded. So researchers use something else.

mock jury: a study involving participants experiencing a trial using a video or transcript, upon which the participant comes to a verdict about the defendant on trial.
大白话：不能进真实陪审团的房间，就让普通人看一段假审判（录像或文字记录），然后让他们判有罪无罪。
这一章几乎所有研究都是 mock jury——这一点后面会变成最重要的评价点（见 §17）。

Other vocabulary you will meet

  • Voir dire（陪审员遴选）— in some countries lawyers may question potential jurors about their suitability, including indirectly about racial bias.
  • Line-up（列队辨认）— a method used by police to help witnesses identify criminals; witnesses are shown a series of images and asked to identify the suspect.
  • Deliberation（评议）— the private discussion in which the jury reviews evidence and reaches a verdict.

The specification lists three factors. They are not three random topics — they arrive at different moments in the same case. Putting them on a timeline makes the whole chapter much easier to hold in your head.

Quick summary

  1. A jury of 12 random people decides guilt — the judge cannot.
  2. Real deliberations are confidential and cannot be recorded, so research uses mock juries.
  3. Factor 1 acts in the courtroom; factor 2 acts before the trial; factor 3 is an attempt to fix juror error.

4. Factor 1a · Race — the Claim

During a trial, jurors can see and hear the defendant. Characteristics such as race, gender, attractiveness and accent can trigger stereotypes that jury members already hold.

The mechanism 机制

A stereotype is a shortcut: it tells us how to categorise a person before we know anything about them. Stereotypes are triggered by how someone sounds, how they look, or how they behave. In a courtroom that shortcut can quietly become a judgement of guilt.

大白话：刻板印象就是「看到某一类人，脑子里自动跳出一个结论」。陪审员不是坏人——他们只是也带着这些自动反应进了法庭。

Why researchers take this seriously

Human Rights Watch (2000) reported that minorities are disproportionately imprisoned in America — a larger share of the prison population than their share of the general population. The same pattern appears elsewhere with marginalised castes and tribal groups. This shows people are treated differently in the justice system because of their race.

Two more things make race especially hard to study:

  • Juries are randomly selected, so most contain a mix of races — but problems arise when the defendant's race differs from the majority of jurors.
  • In countries using voir dire, lawyers may question potential jurors. Selecting jurors directly by race is not permitted, but lawyers can ask indirectly about racial bias.

A common finding from mock trials: white jurors show negative bias to Black defendants, giving harsher sentences and returning more guilty verdicts than for white defendants.

5. Factor 1a · Race — the Evidence

The studies do not all agree — and that disagreement is itself a finding you can use in an exam.

StudyWhat was done and what was found
Bradbury and Williams (2013)Real jury data across several US states. Black defendants were less likely to be convicted by juries with a higher proportion of Black jurors, and more likely to be convicted by juries with a higher proportion of white and Hispanic jurors.
Skolnick and Shaw (1997)Mock jury, students at California State University. Black and white jurors read a murder trial describing a Black or white defendant with high or low celebrity status. Black jurors judged the Black defendant more favourably and gave more lenient sentences; white jurors did not differentiate. Celebrity status had no effect, so race was the main factor. Overall a Black defendant received fewer guilty verdicts regardless of jury race — possibly because white jurors feared being called racist.
Abwender and Hough (2001)Mock jury, 207 participants from education programmes across the USA; Black, white and Hispanic defendants; race and attractiveness both studied. Black participants showed leniency to their own race; Hispanics showed the opposite; white participants showed no ethnic bias.
Mitchell et al. (2005)Meta-analysis of 46 data samples from 34 studies. A small but significant racial bias: participants were more likely to return a guilty verdict when the defendant was a different race from themselves. Bias was stronger when the juror was Black, and was affected by whether the jury received instructions and by the publication date of the study.
把上面四行读成一句话：证据方向不一致。有的研究说黑人被告更吃亏（Bradbury & Williams），有的说黑人被告反而判得更轻（Skolnick & Shaw），有的说白人陪审员根本没区别（Abwender & Hough）。
Mitchell 的元分析是收口：把所有研究放一起看，偏向确实存在但很小，而且取决于条件。
How to use this in an answer. "There is a small but significant racial bias" is a stronger sentence than "juries are racist". Mitchell et al. (2005) gives you the exact wording, and the inconsistent single studies give you a criticism.

6. Factor 1a · Race — Evaluation

✓ Strengths

  • A meta-analysis supports it. Mitchell et al. (2005) combined 46 samples from 34 studies and still found a significant effect — so the bias is not a one-off.
  • Some evidence is real, not mock. Bradbury and Williams (2013) used real conviction data, and Human Rights Watch (2000) documented real imprisonment patterns.
  • Methods converge. Real-data studies, mock juries and meta-analysis point in a broadly similar direction.

✗ Weaknesses

  • Findings are inconsistent. Skolnick and Shaw found leniency; Abwender and Hough found no white bias. Mitchell et al. concluded bias "may not be consistent among racial groups".
  • Mostly mock juries. Students reading a summary do not face a real person's freedom, so the effect may be larger or smaller in reality.
  • Ecological validity. Mitchell et al. found bias was reduced in studies using more realistic procedures, and was not significant when guilty/not-guilty was the measure and jury instructions were given.

An application: can you debias a jury?

Ingriselli (2014) — the debiasing instruction

She tested the impact of a debiasing jury instruction: telling jurors before the trial not to be influenced by bias, prejudice or sympathy. The result — a lower number of guilty verdicts for Black defendants from majority-white juries.

Since the race of a defendant should be irrelevant in any court case, this is psychological knowledge put straight to practical use.

为什么这个例子值钱：考题问「研究有什么用 / 怎么改进司法制度」时，Ingriselli 是唯一一个直接给出解决方案的研究。写评价题时它是现成的 practical application 段。

Two ready-made criticisms of this study

  • It does not reflect what a real jury does. Participants recommended a sentence length, but a real jury only decides guilt — the judge sets the sentence.
  • Attractiveness is subjective. What one juror finds attractive another may not, which makes the variable hard to control.
halo effect: a cognitive bias in which the appearance of a person influences our judgements of their character — for example judging an attractive defendant as more trustworthy.

Accent

StudyWhat was found
Lev-Ari and Keysar (2010)Participants judged the truth of statements spoken by a native speaker (American-English), a mild non-native speaker (Polish, Turkish, German) or a strong non-native speaker (Korean, Turkish, Italian). Strong non-natives were judged less truthful — probably because their speech is harder to process, and difficulty feels like dishonesty.
Dixon and Mahoney (1997)UK study. Participants heard a recording of a suspect talking to a police officer, in either a standard English accent or a regional (Birmingham) accent. The regional accent was rated more guilty.
Seggie (1983)Australian participants heard British, broad Australian or Asian accents across two crimes. Accent interacted with the type of crime: broad Australian was rated more guilty of assault; British was rated more guilty of theft.

8. Factor 2 · What Pre-trial Publicity Is

Internet, television and social media mean a criminal case is often documented for a long time before it reaches court. Members of the public — including future jurors — may form an opinion about the case and any suspects long before the trial begins.

pre-trial publicity (PTP): information about a case and the people in it that reaches potential jurors through the media before the trial starts.

Two mechanisms 两个机制

1 · Schema 图式

A juror's own schema — their mental framework of expectations — influences how they process pre-trial information, and later alters their perception of the evidence presented in court.

2 · Source monitoring error 来源监控错误

Pre-trial publicity can be confused with information presented in court. A juror believes they heard something as evidence when they actually read it in the news — a source monitoring error.

来源监控错误（一句话版）：「我记得这件事，但记错了是从哪儿听来的。」新闻看来的细节，被当成了法庭上听到的证词。
这个术语在上一章（6.1.4 stress & trauma）也出现过——Crombag 的幻影电视画面就是同一个机制。

Two types of publicity 两类报道

Type
What it contains
How long it lasts
Factual 事实型
Incriminating information about the defendant or the case, such as what happened during the crime.
Shorter-lived. As the trial presents more information about what actually happened, factual errors can be corrected.
Emotional 情绪型
No incriminating information, but content that arouses negative emotion — for example the defendant's or victim's past.
Longer-lasting. It is not about facts, so presenting facts in court does not undo it.

9. Factor 2 · Pre-trial Publicity Evidence

StudyWhat was found
Ruva, McEvoy and Bryant (2007)Negative PTP made the defendant seem more guilty and less credible. In their data 73% of collaborating jurors voted guilty, compared with 27% of those exposed to no PTP. The effect was reduced after deliberation — but jurors still confused pre-trial information with case evidence.
Steblay et al. (1999)Meta-analysis of 44 empirical tests representing 5,755 subjects. Negative PTP: 59% guilty versus 45% with less or no negative PTP. Stronger for murder and serious offences, when there were multiple pieces of negative information, and when more time passed between exposure and judgement.
Ogloff and Vidmar (1994)Looked at television as PTP. Potential jurors showed negative bias — and were unaware they held it. Television alone, or television plus print, had more influence than print alone — worrying in an era of 24-hour news.
Kovera (2002)Showed that any media exposure matters, not only negative. Using assault cases with pro-defence, pro-prosecution and neutral coverage: jurors who saw pro-defence coverage said they needed more evidence before convicting than those who saw pro-prosecution coverage.
Ruva and LeVasseur (2012)Content analysis of 30 mock-jury deliberations. PTP changed how evidence was discussed, not just how it was judged. Exposed jurors discussed ambiguous facts in ways that supported the prosecution and rarely in ways that supported the defence — and were unwilling or unable to follow instructions not to mention PTP, rarely correcting others who did.
五个研究怎么记：
Ruva 2007 = 数字最大（73% vs 27%）· Steblay 1999 = 元分析最硬（59% vs 45%）· Ogloff & Vidmar = 电视比报纸更狠 · Kovera = 好报道也会偏（不只是负面）· Ruva 2012 = 评议时也在偏，而且不听指挥。
Scenario questions. The textbook gives you a template: name the study, give the number, then tie it back to the person in the scenario. For example — "Ruva et al. (2007) found negative pre-trial publicity can bias a jury towards a guilty decision, with 73% of collaborating jurors voting guilty compared with 27% of those not exposed; this suggests Lois's client may be convicted because of the news coverage rather than the evidence."

10. Factor 2 · Pre-trial Publicity Evaluation

✓ Strengths

  • Two meta-analyses agree. Steblay et al. (1999, 44 tests, 5,755 subjects) and the broader literature both find a reliable effect.
  • Different methods agree. Experiments (Ruva et al.), content analysis of real discussion (Ruva and LeVasseur) and surveys of potential jurors (Ogloff and Vidmar) all find bias.
  • It explains a real legal problem — which is why courts instruct juries to avoid media coverage in the first place.

✗ Weaknesses

  • Real jurors cannot be deliberately exposed. Researchers cannot show negative publicity to real jurors before they serve, because it might bias them — so nearly all studies are mock juries.
  • Deliberation reduces the effect. Ruva et al. (2007) found the bias was reduced after jury deliberation, which mock studies with individual verdicts may miss.
  • Most studies use students reading a short summary, which is very different from weeks of real news consumption.

What the justice system does about it

Jurors are instructed to avoid media coverage of the case and must not discuss it with others. Where publicity is extreme, a case may be moved to a different area, or the trial delayed. The persistence of the effect in research — and jurors' unawareness of their own bias — is why these safeguards are questioned.

11. Factor 3 · What Expert Testimony Is

expert testimony: evidence given in court by an expert witness, providing objective and unbiased specialist knowledge that may be beyond the understanding of a juror.

Experts may be used to explain complex forensic, clinical, financial or technological evidence. But the type that matters in this chapter is different: an eyewitness expert, who educates the jury about the credibility of witness testimony.

What an eyewitness expert actually says

  • Basic memory processes — for example that memory is reconstructive, not a recording.
  • Factors specific to the case — cross-racial identification, or the impact of stress on memory.

Since mistaken eyewitness accounts are a leading cause of wrongful conviction, alerting jurors to this could prevent miscarriages of justice.

为什么需要专家：陪审员凭常识判断「这个证人可信吗」，但常识对记忆是错的——多数人以为记忆像录像。专家的作用就是把心理学发现搬进法庭。
下一节的问题是：搬进去了，陪审员真的会用吗？

The controversy 争议

  • Critics argue experts can be biased in their opinion, or can unfairly bias a jury against a witness.
  • Others point out that safeguards already exist — cross-examination of the witness and jury instructions — so an expert is unnecessary.
  • Researchers reply that those safeguards are ineffective.

12. Factor 3 · Does Expert Testimony Actually Help?

StudyWhat was found
Jones et al. (2017)Mock jury, 452 participants, comparing jury instructions with expert testimony. Jurors were already alert to the quality of witnessing and identification conditions on their own. Expert testimony raised scepticism and made them less likely to convict regardless of the conditions. Conclusion: neither instructions nor expert testimony helped jurors tell good evidence from bad — expert testimony lowers trust but does not improve discrimination.
Benton et al. (2006)Surveyed 111 ordinary people and compared them with experts. 60% of ordinary people believed memory can be influenced by post-event information, versus 95% of experts. 30% of ordinary people believed misidentification can occur, versus 81% of experts. Ordinary people are therefore more trusting of eyewitnesses than research would advise — evidence that an expert could be useful.
Helm (2021)Mock jury, murder case with strong or weak witness testimony; jurors given no instructions, general information about memory, or detailed information about false memory. Detailed false-memory information reduced guilty verdicts when the evidence was weak, but not when it was strong. So expert information does not make jurors blindly sceptical — it helps them spot weak evidence.
三个研究的张力（必考的评价素材）：
Benton：普通人不懂记忆 → 专家有必要。
Jones：专家没用——只是让陪审员整体更怀疑，分辨不出好坏证据。
Helm：专家有用——证据弱时确实少判有罪，证据强时不变。
结论写法：专家证词的效果取决于给的是什么信息，不是「有或没有专家」。

Helm's own limitations (useful for evaluation)

  • Mock jurors read a simplified transcript and heard no cross-examination.
  • They knew a guilty verdict would not really send anyone to prison.
  • They had no opportunity to deliberate with other jurors.

13. Penrod and Cutler (1989) · Study One

The specification names this paper directly, so you need all three studies. They were built in order: each one answers the question the previous one raised.

Figure 2 — Penrod and Cutler (1989): each study sets up the next.

Study One in detail

Study One was a quantitative re-analysis of existing experiments into eyewitness identification. The question was whether research on human memory is good enough to draw valid conclusions about real-life eyewitness testimony. If it is not, an expert witness in court would be useless — and could even impair the jury.

What they actually re-examined

They re-analysed the meta-analysis by Shapiro and Penrod (1986): 128 eyewitness identification and facial recognition experiments involving 16,950 participants, covering factors such as cross-racial identification and context reinstatement.

The finding

Several factors had reliable effects on eyewitness memory in the laboratory and in field settings. So laboratory-based memory research can explain eyewitness memory — which gives grounds for using expert testimony in court.

逻辑链（写答案时照着走）：实验室研究可靠 → 专家讲的东西是真的 → 专家证词有存在理由。
这一研究本身没碰陪审团，它是给后面两个研究铺路的。

14. Penrod and Cutler (1989) · Study Two

Study Two asked whether jurors are aware of the factors that make an identification unreliable. Mock jurors watched realistic videotaped court trials of an armed robbery in which the central evidence was the victim's positive identification of the robber.

DesignDetail
Participants321 students and 129 jury-eligible or experienced jurors.
MaterialsA 45-minute trial video closely resembling a real trial: opening statements, examination and cross-examination, closing arguments, judge's instructions.
The four witnessesThe store clerk (victim, identified the robber in a line-up, 80% confident); a police officer (conditions of the identification); a friend of the defendant (weak alibi); the defendant (denied the charges).
Ten factors manipulatedAcross 64 versions: hat or no hat; weapon pointed or concealed; victim threatened and pushed or calm demand; identification after 2 or 14 days; mugshot search in between; chance to reject the line-up; line-up of 6 or 12; members similar in appearance; heard the robber speak or not; 80% or 100% confidence.
Dependent variablesGuilty or not guilty; probability the identification was correct; perceived strength of the case; fairness of the line-up; how frightened the witness was; probability an average person could have made the identification.

The finding — and why it is alarming

Jurors were not significantly influenced by these ten factors. Of all ten, only witness confidence had a statistically significant effect on verdicts and on judged accuracy.

So jurors are insensitive to the factors that actually influence eyewitness identification — they are naïve to potential witness errors. Worse, they place disproportionate weight on witness confidence, which is not a useful predictor of accuracy.

一句话版：陪审员对真正重要的条件（有没有蒙脸、隔了几天、列队公不公平）完全无感，却对最没用的东西（证人有多自信）极度敏感。
这就是为什么要请专家——§15 测试专家能不能修好这个问题。

A useful extra finding

There was no difference between the students and the eligible or experienced jurors. Penrod and Cutler concluded that evidence from student populations can be generalised to all potential jury members — a rare piece of good news for mock jury research.

15. Penrod and Cutler (1989) · Study Three

Studies One and Two established two things: laboratory research can explain eyewitness memory, and potential jurors are unaware of the factors that affect it. Study Three tested the fix: would expert testimony make jurors more sensitive?

Cutler, Penrod and Dexter used 538 students who watched the same trial variations as Study Two — this time with expert eyewitness testimony added.

What the expert did

  • Explained the reconstructive nature of memory.
  • Educated the jury on stress, weapon presence, line-up procedures, and the confidence–accuracy relationship.
  • Was then cross-examined by the prosecution, which tried to discredit him — claiming he had only ever testified for the defence, and that psychological research cannot be applied to real eyewitness testimony.

The findings

  • Expert testimony improved juror sensitivity to witnessing and identification conditions — jurors took those factors into account more.
  • Jurors gave less weight to witness confidence — exactly the problem Study Two identified.
  • Descriptive testimony was more effective than descriptive and quantitative testimony.
  • No scepticism effect: neither the presence nor the type of expert testimony affected credibility ratings of the witness or the verdict.
「no scepticism effect」为什么重要：批评者说专家会让陪审员无差别怀疑所有证人（见 §12 的 Jones）。Penrod 和 Cutler 说不会——专家让陪审员更会分辨，而不是更怀疑。
另一个反直觉发现：信息不是越多越好。纯描述比「描述 + 数据」更有效。

✓ Strengths

  • Three studies that build on each other. Study One justifies the research, Study Two finds the problem, Study Three tests the solution.
  • High ecological validity for a mock jury. A 45-minute trial video with cross-examination and judge's instructions is far more realistic than a written summary.
  • Two samples in Study Two. Students and jury-eligible/experienced jurors produced the same judgements, supporting generalisability.
  • Practical application. The findings tell courts which form of expert testimony helps.

✗ Weaknesses

  • Still mock juries. Participants knew no one would go to prison, and in Study Three there was no deliberation.
  • Student samples dominate — Study Three used 538 students only.
  • Conflicting evidence. Jones et al. (2017) found expert testimony did not help jurors discriminate good from bad evidence, contradicting Study Three.
  • Artificial discrediting. The cross-examination that attacked the expert was staged, so real courtroom dynamics may differ.

17. How Good Is Mock Jury Research?

This section is your evaluation toolkit. Almost every study in this chapter is a mock jury — so the strengths and weaknesses of that method can be attached to any of them.

IssueThe evidence about the issue
Students are not representativeBornstein (1999) meta-analysis of mock jury research: there is little difference between the decisions of student and non-student mock juries. Where differences appear, students tend to be more lenient.
Written summaries are unrealisticNemeth (1977) found no difference in decision-making between trials presented as a written summary and trials staged live — reassuring, because it means the format alone does not change the outcome.
No real consequencesA real jury decides whether a person goes to prison; a mock juror does not. This may affect how seriously the task is taken, so laboratory findings lack the realism of a real trial.
Too few variablesLaboratory research isolates variables well, but real jurors face a wider range of influences at once.
Real jurors can be askedReal jury members have completed self-report questionnaires after trials. Self-report has limitations, but it is a rare chance to study real decision-making, and is likely more reliable than a mock trial: real jurors report what they did decide, whereas a mock trial only records what a participant would do.
Different countries differLegal processes vary — some US states use the death penalty, which may make a guilty verdict feel different. Findings from one jurisdiction may not transfer to another.
评价题万能三组：
① 样本——学生不代表公众（但 Bornstein 说差别不大，学生还更宽容）。
② 生态效度——文字摘要 / 没有真实后果 / 没有评议（但 Nemeth 说形式本身不影响结果）。
③ 跨文化——法律制度不同，美国有死刑，结论不能直接搬。

Question 1 · Mock jury method (4 + 4 marks)

ZsaZsi used a mock jury study to investigate the impact of witness confidence on jurors' perception of the accuracy of eyewitness testimony. She created two videos of the same murder trial, one where the witness stated they were 70% confident and one where they were 100% confident. She gathered participants from her university common room to watch the video and rate whether the murderer was guilty or not guilty.

a) Explain two strengths of ZsaZsi using a mock jury research method. (4 marks)
b) Explain two weaknesses of ZsaZsi using a mock jury research method. (4 marks)

Model answer

a) Strengths. First, a mock jury allows the researcher to control variables — ZsaZsi can keep the trial identical and change only witness confidence, so she can be confident that confidence caused any difference in verdicts. Second, Bornstein (1999) found little difference between student and non-student mock juries, so using students from a common room may still generalise to real jurors; Penrod and Cutler (1989) also found no difference between students and jury-eligible jurors.

b) Weaknesses. First, low ecological validity: participants know a guilty verdict will not send anyone to prison, so they may not take the decision as seriously as a real jury, and here there was no deliberation at all — yet Ruva et al. (2007) found deliberation changes the effect of bias. Second, the sample is students from one common room, which is not representative of the population; Bornstein notes that where differences do appear, students tend to be more lenient, which could underestimate guilty verdicts.

Why this scores: every point says what the problem is and why it matters for the conclusion.

Question 2 · Describe pre-trial publicity (3 marks)

Describe pre-trial publicity as a factor that influences jury decision-making.

Model answer

  • What it is: pre-trial publicity is information about a case that reaches potential jurors through the media — internet, television, newspapers and social media — before the trial begins.
  • Two types: factual publicity contains incriminating information about the case, whereas emotional publicity arouses negative emotion, for example about the defendant's past. Emotional publicity is held to have a longer-lasting influence, because factual errors can be corrected as the trial presents evidence.
  • How it works: it can cause a source monitoring error, where a juror believes they heard something as evidence in court when they actually read it in the media.

Question 3 · Justify race (2 marks)

Justify race as a factor that affects jury decision-making.

Model answer

Race is justified as a factor because Mitchell et al. (2005) carried out a meta-analysis of 46 data samples from 34 studies and found a small but significant effect: participants were more likely to return a guilty verdict when the defendant was a different race from themselves. This is supported by real-world data — Bradbury and Williams (2013) found Black defendants were more likely to be convicted by juries with a higher proportion of white and Hispanic jurors.

Why this scores: two marks means two pieces of evidence, and both are named with numbers.

Question 4 · Evaluate Penrod and Cutler (1989) (8 marks)

Evaluate Penrod and Cutler's (1989) research into eyewitness expert testimony.

¶What goes in it
1Study One: re-analysis of Shapiro and Penrod (1986) — 128 experiments, 16,950 participants; lab findings hold in the field, so expert testimony has grounds.
2Study Two: 321 students plus 129 eligible/experienced jurors; ten factors across 64 versions; only witness confidence mattered — jurors are naïve.
3Study Three: 538 students; expert testimony improved sensitivity to conditions, reduced weight on confidence, descriptive better than quantitative, no scepticism effect.
4Evaluation: strengths (ecological validity, two samples, builds logically) against weaknesses (still mock, students, contradicted by Jones et al. 2017).

Model answer — four developed points

  • Study One justifies the approach. Penrod and Cutler re-analysed Shapiro and Penrod's (1986) meta-analysis of 128 eyewitness identification and facial recognition experiments involving 16,950 participants. They found several factors — such as cross-racial identification and context reinstatement — had reliable effects in both laboratory and field settings, so laboratory memory research can explain real eyewitness memory. This gives grounds for using expert testimony in court.
  • Study Two finds the problem. Mock jurors watched realistic 45-minute videotaped armed robbery trials; 321 students and 129 jury-eligible or experienced jurors saw ten factors manipulated across 64 versions, such as whether a weapon was visible and whether identification took place after 2 or 14 days. Only witness confidence significantly affected verdicts, showing jurors are insensitive to the factors that actually influence identification accuracy and place disproportionate weight on confidence, which does not predict accuracy.
  • Study Three tests the solution. Cutler, Penrod and Dexter used 538 students who saw the same variations with expert testimony added, and the expert was discredited in cross-examination. Expert testimony improved juror sensitivity to witnessing and identification conditions and reduced the weight given to confidence; descriptive testimony was more effective than descriptive and quantitative testimony, and there was no scepticism effect on credibility ratings or verdicts.
  • Evaluation. A strength is that the three studies build logically on each other, and the trial footage is far more realistic than a written summary; using both students and experienced jurors also supports generalisability. However, these remain mock juries with no real consequences and no deliberation, and Study Three used students only. Most seriously, Jones et al. (2017), with 452 participants, found that expert testimony raised scepticism without helping jurors distinguish good from bad evidence — directly contradicting Study Three. This means the value of expert testimony remains contested.

Why this scores: all three studies are described with numbers, and the fourth paragraph weighs evidence against evidence rather than just listing criticisms.

🔑 Exam key points — what a strong answer contains ›
  1. The jury decides guilt — 12 random people; the judge cannot. Deliberation is confidential and cannot be recorded, so researchers use mock juries.
  2. Race: Mitchell et al. (2005) meta-analysis — 46 samples, 34 studies, small but significant bias when defendant and juror differ in race. Single studies disagree (Skolnick and Shaw: leniency; Abwender and Hough: no white bias).
  3. Attractiveness: Sigall and Ostrove (1975) — attractive photo meant longer sentences for fraud but shorter for burglary. Criticise it: juries do not set sentences, and attractiveness is subjective.
  4. Pre-trial publicity: factual versus emotional; source monitoring error. Steblay et al. (1999): 59% versus 45%. Ruva et al. (2007): 73% versus 27%, reduced after deliberation.
  5. Expert testimony: Benton et al. (2006) shows ordinary people under-understand memory (60% vs 95%; 30% vs 81%); Jones et al. (2017) says it does not help discrimination; Helm (2021) says it helps with weak evidence.
  6. Penrod and Cutler: Study One justifies, Study Two finds jurors naïve (only confidence mattered), Study Three shows expert testimony improves sensitivity with no scepticism effect.
  7. Every criticism needs a why it matters sentence — and the recurring ones here are mock juries, student samples and cross-country legal differences.
  8. In scenario answers, first ask: did this influence arrive before the trial or during it?

20. Key Terms

jury陪审团
A group, typically of 12, randomly selected from an electoral roll, who decide whether the defendant is guilty.
deliberation评议
The private stage where the jury reviews the evidence and discusses the case to reach a verdict; confidential and never recorded.
foreperson陪审团发言人
The jury member appointed to present the jury's decision back to the judge.
voir dire陪审员遴选
A process in some countries where lawyers may question the suitability of potential jurors, including indirectly about racial bias.
line-up列队辨认
A method used by police to help witnesses identify criminals by showing a series of images.
mock jury模拟陪审团
A study in which participants experience a trial through a video or transcript and then reach a verdict.
stereotype刻板印象
A belief about the characteristics of a group, which can bias how an individual defendant is judged.
halo effect光环效应
A cognitive bias in which a person's appearance influences judgements of their character.
pre-trial publicity审前报道
Media information about a case that reaches potential jurors before the trial begins.
factual publicity事实型报道
Publicity containing incriminating information about the case; can be corrected as the trial presents evidence.
emotional publicity情绪型报道
Publicity that arouses negative emotion rather than stating facts; considered longer-lasting than factual publicity.
source monitoring error来源监控错误
Mistaking where information was learned, so media reports are remembered as court evidence.
schema图式
A mental framework of expectations that shapes how pre-trial information and court evidence are interpreted.
expert testimony专家证词
Evidence from an expert witness giving specialist knowledge beyond a juror's understanding.
debiasing instruction去偏见指示
An instruction telling jurors not to be influenced by bias, prejudice or sympathy (Ingriselli, 2014).
meta-analysis元分析
A statistical combination of many studies, e.g. Mitchell et al. (2005); Steblay et al. (1999); Bornstein (1999).
content analysis内容分析
A method of analysing what people actually say, e.g. Ruva and LeVasseur's (2012) analysis of 30 deliberations.
ecological validity生态效度
Whether findings from artificial settings, such as a mock trial, apply to real courtrooms.
miscarriage of justice司法误判
A wrongful conviction; mistaken eyewitness accounts are a leading cause.
