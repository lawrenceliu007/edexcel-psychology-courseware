6.1.4 FACTORS INFLUENCING EYEWITNESS MEMORY
目击证词可靠性的影响因素

Criminological Psychology · post-event information（事件后信息）· stress & trauma（压力与创伤）· weapon focus（武器聚焦）· other-race effect（异族效应）
🧠 Core idea: memory is evidence — and evidence can fail 🔑 Four factors, one question: can you trust a witness? 📖 Textbook: Chapter 9

1. Getting Started

🎪 The concert Textbook scenario

You are at a loud outdoor concert in a public park with friends. It is getting dark; the stage lights flicker on and off. A fight breaks out. Someone gets hurt, security guards rush over, and panic spreads. They carry the victim away. A guard reaches your group and asks: What did you see?

Before reading anything else — tick everything in this story that might make remembering harder:

这一章的全部内容就藏在这个场景里：天黑、灯光闪 = 看不清（压力太大影响记忆）；吓到了 = stress & trauma；事后大家一直讨论 = post-event information。而"保安来问话"之所以重要，是因为你的回答会变成法庭上的证据。这一章要回答一个问题：目击证人的记忆，到底有多可靠？什么会把它弄坏？

2. Why Eyewitnesses Matter

eyewitness（目击证人） — an individual who sees an event, such as a crime, occurring.

eyewitness testimony（目击证人证词） — the account an eyewitness gives of what they saw, usually to the police and later in court.

An eyewitness sees a crime happen. The police interview them, they document everything they can remember, and later they may be required to attend court and recount the event to the judge and jury. The eyewitness is often the most common form of evidence in criminal trials — and juries tend to find it very convincing.

❗ The problem

Our memories are not as accurate as we might want or expect them to be. Incorrect eyewitness accounts have been identified as the leading cause of miscarriages of justice（司法误判）. An innocent person can be convicted because a witness — honest, confident, and completely wrong — picked them out.

为什么这一章值得学：目击证人是法庭上最常见、也最被陪审团相信的证据。但"我很确定"和"我是对的"是两回事。诚实 ≠ 准确 —— 证人没有说谎，只是记忆本身被弄坏了。这一章就是研究弄坏记忆的四种方式。

📎 One more thing before we start

You already know one tool designed to protect eyewitness memory: the cognitive interview（认知访谈，spec 6.1.2）。It uses open, non-leading questions precisely because leading questions can contaminate memory. Everything in this chapter explains why that technique matters.

4. Factor 1 · Post-event Information

post-event information（事件后信息） — any information after the witnessed event which may influence the original memory of the incident.

leading question（引导性问题） — a question that suggests the answer it wants, e.g. "How tall was the person?" implies the person was tall.

Post-event information can introduce new information that gets incorporated into the memory, or it can alter the original memory. It can even activate schemas（图式）— expectations about what usually happens in an event — which then alter how the incident is recalled later.

Where does it come from? 事后信息从哪来？

  • Talking to other witnesses — discussions before the police interview
  • Being questioned by the police or lawyers — leading questions, sometimes used unintentionally, sometimes deliberately in court to confuse a witness
  • Watching media coverage — especially if the case is high-profile

And this matters because witnesses are interviewed over a period of time, and it can take a long time before a case goes to court. What the witness experiences in that gap changes what they remember.

🌳 The mechanism: reconstructive memory 重构记忆

We do not replay memories like video. Reconstructive memory means we rebuild the scene using the main points and our interpretation — influenced by our cultural norms and expectations. That is why post-event information can blend in so smoothly: the memory is already a reconstruction, so new details slot right in. (This links back to encoding and schemas, Student Book 1, page 96.)

一个类比：记忆不像硬盘里的录像文件，更像一篇每次重讲都会改写的作文。你朋友说了一句"那人戴了帽子吧？"——下次你回忆时，"帽子"可能就真的出现在你的画面里了。不是你撒谎，是作文被补写了一笔。misinformation effect（错误信息效应）：记忆因为接触事后信息而变得不准确。

5. The Lab Evidence 实验室证据

Gabbert, Memon and Allen (2003) — memory conformity 记忆从众

  • 120 students watched a video of a girl returning a book to a university office — placed in pairs, and each partner saw a slightly different version. One saw the book returned; the other saw the girl stealing it.
  • One condition recalled without discussion; the other discussed first.
  • 71% of witnesses who discussed recalled items that were not in their own video but came from the discussion.
  • 60% who discussed said the girl was guilty even though their own version never showed a theft.
这个实验妙在哪：两位"目击者"看的是不同的视频——所以讨论后说出对方视频里的细节，就能证明信息是从讨论里来的，不是从记忆里来的。这叫 memory conformity（记忆从众）：讨论让证词变得像一个模子刻出来的。

Poole and Lindsay (1995) — children and leading questions 儿童与引导性问题

  • Children aged 3–4 and 5–7 took part in a science activity with a character called Mr Science. Initially interviewed with no misinformation, they were generally accurate.
  • Three months later, parents read them a story about Mr Science that included two activities the child never did.
  • In a second interview with misleading questions: 95% of children answered at least one leading question incorrectly about an activity they had not experienced.
  • They could not distinguish what they experienced from what they were read in the story.

Most post-event research uses adults — but children are highly suggestible（易受暗示的）when post-event information arrives as leading questions.

读研究的技巧：每个实验记三样就够 —— 谁（被试）· 做了什么（ manipulated 什么）· 得到什么数字。Gabbert 记 71% / 60%，Poole & Lindsay 记 95%。数字就是答案里的"Evidence"。

6. The Real-life Evidence 真实世界证据

Laboratory findings look scary. But witnesses of real crimes sometimes hold up much better — and that contradiction is exactly what an evaluation question wants from you.

Yuille and Cutshall (1986) — a real shooting

Case study of a real shop robbery and shooting in Canada. Real witnesses were interviewed about the incident, and their accounts were compared with police records. The witnesses had detailed memories and were not misled by two leading questions — about a "busted headlight" (it was not broken) and a "yellow quarter panel" (it was blue).

Odinot (2008) — a real supermarket robbery

28 employees of a Dutch supermarket were interviewed three months after two armed robbers hit the safe. Compared with CCTV footage, 84% of remembered information was accurate. Many had watched a televised reconstruction five weeks later, containing errors — yet it did not alter their memories.

两条线并排记：实验室说"事后信息会污染记忆"（Gabbert 71% 被带偏）；真实案件说"未必"（Yuille & Cutshall 没被带偏，Odinot 84% 准确）。谁对？大概率都对——区别在场景：实验室里看视频不紧张、不投入；真实罪案里证人亲眼在场，记得牢。真实世界的研究生态效度更高，实验室的控制更好——这是 7 节评价的核心。

⚠️ But read the small print 细节也很重要

Field studies have limits too: Yuille and Cutshall used only a small sample of witnesses, and it is likely Odinot's witnesses reconciled（互相核对纠正）any errors from the TV reconstruction during discussions at work — so the misinformation may simply never have survived.

7. Evaluating Factor 1 · Post-event Information

Support 支持

The Loftus misinformation effect

Elizabeth Loftus has pioneered eyewitness research since the 1970s. Her misinformation effect: memory becomes less accurate because of exposure to post-event information.

Evidence a large body of research supports the claim that post-event information influences eyewitness reliability.

It explains real harm

False convictions have been traced to contaminated memory. If leading questions are detected, the testimony can be ruled inadmissible; if not, false testimony can convict a suspect.

Application the cognitive interview (open, non-leading questions) is a direct fix built on this research.

Criticisms 批评

Laboratory vs real life — psychology as a science

Lab studies yield different results from real-life witnesses. Lab experiments arguably minimise emotional stress — participants know the crime is not real — so they may be less invested in remembering. High control creates an artificial environment: no noise, no real danger, no real stakes.

Ecological validity Why it matters: the lab may overstate how fragile real memories are — real witnesses (Yuille and Cutshall; Odinot) resisted contamination.

Ethical issues 伦理问题

Investigating eyewitness memory can involve exposing participants to a traumatic event or film footage that may cause distress. Using real witnesses to a crime raises another issue: questioning forces them to relive the incident.

Ethics Why it matters: protection from psychological harm limits what researchers are allowed to do.

评价的写作套路（和 1.1.1 一样）：每条 = 说什么有问题 + 为什么这是个问题。"实验室结果和真实案件不一致"只算半句；补上"因为实验室过度控制、被试知道不是真的，可能夸大了记忆的脆弱性"，才是完整评价。

8. Factor 2 · Stress and the Mind

arousal（唤醒） — the level of physiological and psychological alertness a person is experiencing, from sleepy to highly stressed.

Yerkes–Dodson Law（耶克斯–多德森定律）(1908) — performance increases with arousal up to an optimum point, then declines as arousal gets too high.

Eyewitnesses are placed under great emotional stress when witnessing an event. Research suggests that as we become stressed about a situation, our performance — including remembering information — is impaired when we get too aroused. Being too relaxed does not help either: there is an optimum amount of arousal, and it is very easy to go beyond it.

Figure 2 · The inverted-U: a little stress helps, too much wrecks performance

Three ideas in this section 三个机制

① The Yerkes–Dodson Law

Optimum arousal exists; a very stressful event pushes arousal past the optimum, and recall of the incident is impaired.

② Attentional narrowing

Easterbrook hypothesis (1959): under high emotional arousal, attention narrows. Stressed people focus on fewer details and do not encode the rest — like a camera zooming in and losing the edges of the frame.

③ Source monitoring problems

Traumatic events get rehearsed — police statements, media, conversations. People may confuse information encoded after the event with what actually happened, because they fail to distinguish the source of the information. Traumatic memories are therefore more prone to distortion.

三者怎么连起来：压力太大（Yerkes–Dodson 过了顶点）→ 注意变窄（Easterbrook，只盯住核心细节，边缘信息根本没存进去）→ 事后反复回想、看电视、聊天（来源监控失灵，把"后来听说的"当成"亲眼看过的"）。注意"主观感觉"这个陷阱：证人往往觉得自己记忆栩栩如生（vivid memory），但压力恰恰损害了编码——感觉越真实，越要警惕。

📺 The phantom footage Crombag et al. (1996)

A Boeing 747 crashed into apartment buildings in Amsterdam. Soon afterwards, researchers asked people: did you see the television footage of the moment of impact? What would you say? Pick one:

为什么这个实验经典：根本没有电视拍过撞击画面。但 60% 的被试声称自己看过。他们不是撒谎——创伤性事件让记忆特别容易被事后信息（谈话、想象、媒体）污染，来源监控完全失灵。这直接演示了机制 ③。

9. Stress Evidence 压力研究的证据

Lab and field: stress impairs recall 实验证据：压力损害记忆

Morgan III et al. (2004) — survival training

509 people in a survival training exercise: each experienced either a low-stress or high-stress interrogation lasting over 30 minutes, then tried to identify their interrogator in a line-up or photo identification. A large number in the high-stress condition could not make a correct identification.

Morgan III et al. (2007) — the follow-up

53 personnel exposed to acute interrogation stress in a mock captivity exercise: 1 in 3 incorrectly identified their interrogator 48 hours later. Facial recognition is impaired by stress and trauma.

Valentine and Mesout (2009) — the London Dungeon

A classic study into arousal and recall: visitors to the London Dungeon were genuinely stressed by the horror attraction — participants did not know they would be asked to recall what they saw. Eyewitness identification was impaired by arousal.

Deffenbacher et al. (2004) — the meta-analysis

Taken as a whole body of research: stress has more of a negative impact on eyewitness recall than a positive one — true for both eyewitness identification of a perpetrator and recall of event details.

Real life: stress sometimes leaves memory intact 真实世界：记忆有时完好

Thompson et al. (1997) — the Marchioness

Survivors of the Marchioness ferry sinking, a highly traumatic event: 74 out of 86 statements were confirmed as accurate.

Rinolo et al. (2003) — the Titanic

Survivor statements of the Titanic sinking: 15 out of 20 eyewitnesses reported the ship breaking apart while on the surface — consistent with forensic evidence.

Yuille and Cutshall (1986) — again

Witnesses who reported high stress during the shooting recalled with greater accuracy than those who reported less. But note the catch: those most stressed were also closest to the incident — that may account for their greater accuracy.

考试最爱的"但是"：实验室一边倒说压力有害，真实幸存者却记得很准。Deffenbacher (2004) 的元分析给出裁决：总体上压力是负面的。为什么结果不一致？—— 有趣的方法学批评：有的研究里的唤醒来自焦虑和恐惧（像真目击者），有的只来自警觉和注意（实验室条件）。"arousal"这个词在不同研究里含义不同，所以比较研究很困难。这句话写进 10 分/12 分题就是高级评价点。

10. Evaluating Factor 2 · Stress and Trauma

Support 支持

The meta-analysis settles it

Deffenbacher et al. (2004) shows that across the whole research base, stress impairs both identification and recall of event details.

Evidence many separate studies point the same way.

Real witnesses in real settings

Valentine and Mesout (2009) caught genuine arousal — participants did not know they would be tested. Morgan's participants faced genuinely stressful interrogations.

Evidence some studies do capture real stress rather than a classroom imitation.

Criticisms 批评

Laboratory arousal is not eyewitness arousal

Lab studies of this area may not reach the critical level of arousal — participants know what they see is not real, and the artificial setting produces under-arousal. Arousal could mean anxiety or fear (as for real witnesses) or simply heightened alertness (in the lab). This confusion in the term arousal makes comparing studies complex.

Validity Why it matters: the experiments may measure the wrong thing entirely.

Real-life studies contradict the lab — and each other

Thompson (74/86 accurate), Rinolo (15/20 accurate) and Yuille and Cutshall (high stress = more accurate) clash with Morgan and Deffenbacher. And Yuille and Cutshall's most stressed witnesses were also the closest to the incident — stress was tangled up with viewing distance.

Contradictory findings Why it matters: a factor that helps in one study and harms in another has not yet been pinned down.

📌 Practical issues in research 设计与实施的实际问题

The Yerkes–Dodson curve warns researchers: lab participants may sit in the under-aroused flat part of the curve while a real witness sits past the peak. You cannot ethically create a genuine trauma in a laboratory — so the very studies that are most controlled are the least true to life. This trade-off reappears in every factor of this chapter.

11. Factor 3 · Weapon Focus

weapon focus（武器聚焦） — refers to an eyewitness's concentration on a weapon to the exclusion of other details of a crime.

One specific factor proven to affect recall of an event — and therefore eyewitness accuracy — is whether a weapon was used in the offence. There are two possible explanations, and being able to name both is what the exam rewards:

Figure 3 · Two routes, one outcome — you remember the weapon and not the perpetrator
两条路线的区别是考点：路线 1 说"记忆差是因为吓"（stress，接 Yerkes–Dodson）；路线 2 说"记忆差是因为只顾看"（attention，不需要害怕也会发生）。哪条对？下一节 Pickel 的生鸡肉实验会给你答案 —— 高威胁但平常的东西（剪刀）不伤记忆，低威胁但奇怪的东西（生鸡肉）伤记忆 → unusualness 奇怪程度比 threat 威胁程度更像元凶。

12. Weapon Focus Evidence

Loftus et al. (1987) — the eye-tracking experiment

36 participants watched a series of slides of a customer in a restaurant. One version: the customer was armed; the other: the same customer held a chequebook. Eye movements were recorded. Participants who saw the weapon tended to focus their gaze on the weapon. As a result they were less likely to identify the customer in an identity parade (11% vs 39%) and had a worse memory for the event.

Maass and Köhnken (1989) — the field experiment

86 students were approached by a woman holding either a pen or a syringe. When asked to identify the woman from a line-up, two-thirds of participants in the syringe condition made an incorrect identification — supporting the weapon effect in a more real-life situation.

Pickel (1998) — is it threat or unusualness? 威胁还是奇怪？

Video of a hair salon: a man walks up to the receptionist and hands her money. Only the object in his hand changes. Participants completed a 10-minute filler task, then answered recall questions. She found the presence of either a weapon or raw chicken produced the poorest recall of the man, while the wallet and scissors had less effect.

🧪 Your prediction Quick check

Both the weapon and the scissors are high-threat items — yet the scissors did not damage recall (you would expect them to in a hair salon… or not?). Which conclusion did Pickel draw?

这张 2×2 矩阵是本章最好用的工具：纵轴"奇不奇怪"，横轴"吓不吓人"。伤记忆的是奇怪的那两个（武器、生鸡肉），不奇怪的两个（钱包、剪刀）都还行 → unusualness（奇怪程度）才是拉开注意力的东西。Pickel 认为正是武器的"不寻常"抓住了我们的注意。记住：生鸡肉听起来荒谬，但它在实验里和武器一样有效 —— 这就是它经典的原因。

Pickel et al. (2006) — can training overcome it? 训练能克服吗？

Participants watched a staged scene in a small classroom, first hearing one of two lectures: (a) about weapon focus — teaching them to attend to perpetrator features instead of dwelling on any weapon; or (b) about eyewitness confidence (neutral). A man then burst in carrying either a book or a weapon. For those given lecture (b), the weapon produced fewer correct details. But those who had the weapon-focus lecture were protected: object type had no effect on them. Pickel concluded that with proper instruction, the weapon-focus effect could be overcome.

13. Evaluating Factor 3 · Weapon Focus

Support 支持

Multiple methods agree

Lab eye-tracking (Loftus 1987), field approach (Maass and Köhnken 1989) and simulation (Pickel 1998) all show poorer identification or recall when an unusual object appears.

Evidence the effect survives changes of method.

The meta-analysis confirms it

Fawcett et al. (2013) concluded that weapon presence consistently demonstrated a negative effect on both feature accuracy and identification accuracy under controlled conditions and in real-life situations.

Evidence the strongest form of support — a whole body of research.

Criticisms 批评

Real police data found no effect

Wagstaff et al. (2003) coded real police interviews of witnesses and victims of robberies and assaults (two UK police forces) and compared them against the primary suspect's description. They found no evidence of any effect of weapon presence on feature accuracy. Yuille and Cutshall's real witnesses were not affected by the weapon either.

Ecological validity Why it matters: the laboratory effect may simply not appear in real crimes.

Lab slides are not real crimes

Loftus's slides do not emulate the real experience of being in a restaurant and seeing a weapon. And Fawcett notes real-world research is complicated by factors labs control easily — such as how long the witness was exposed to the weapon.

Artificiality Why it matters: a slide of a weapon is not a threat to you.

🌍 Wider issue: psychology in society 心理学知识在社会中的应用

Pickel's studies suggest we should carefully consider the reliability of an eyewitness in court if the crime involved a weapon — and recent studies suggest the effect can be overcome. It has particular application to police officers, who routinely face these situations: if officers can be trained in the area of weapon focus, their recall of events when a weapon was present could improve.

📝 Exam tip（课本原文要点）

If you are using a number of studies to evaluate a topic — such as weapon focus — it is not always necessary to describe every stage of each study in detail. The emphasis of your written work is on showing your understanding of the studies and using that to evaluate whether they support the topic or not.

14. Factor 4 · The Other-race Effect

other-race effect / cross-race effect（异族效应） — same-race faces are more accurately recognised than faces of a different race.

in-group（内群体） — a group to which we have membership.

out-group（外群体） — a group to which we do not have membership.

The difficulty in recognising other-race faces compared to own-race faces is one of the most researched issues in criminological research on eyewitness identification. It matters because since the advent of DNA testing, we now know many people were convicted of crimes they did not commit based on false eyewitness identification.

⛓️ Ronald Cotton (1984) — the case that defines the issue

In 1984, a white woman, Jennifer Thompson, was assaulted by a man who gained entry to her apartment. Despite considerable effort to remember the face of the Black assailant, she misidentified Ronald Cotton. He was convicted of assault and spent more than ten years in prison before being exonerated — because of later DNA evidence. Thompson was a motivated, honest witness who tried her hardest. She was still wrong.

Two explanations 两个解释

① Encoding differences — Valentine (1991)

We encode faces by attending to features that discriminate between faces of our own race. We are experienced at discriminating own-race facial features, but not other-race ones. Problem: this fails to explain why the effect occurs even in individuals with high exposure to other-race faces.

② In-group / out-group processing — social-cognitive theories

We quickly group individuals as in-group or out-group members. In-group faces are visually processed as individuals — discriminated one from another. Out-group faces are only processed as a way to categorise them. Prediction: own-race faces are encoded qualitatively better.

两个解释一句话区分：解释 ① 说"练习问题"——你练的是分辨本族面孔的能力；解释 ② 说"归类问题"——你把外族人脸当成"一类"，把本族人脸当成"一个人"。注意 ① 的漏洞：如果只是练习，那接触外族人多的人就不该有异族效应 —— 但证据（下一节 Sangrigoli 的收养研究）显示练习/接触确实能减弱效应，这恰好反过来支持了"经验可以改变编码"。

15. Other-race Evidence and Evaluation

The evidence 证据

Study 研究Findings 发现
Scheck, Neufeld and Dwyer (2000)Looked at 77 mistaken eyewitness identifications: 35% were white victim witnesses misidentifying Black suspects — the other-race effect influences eyewitness reliability.
Smith (2004)161 white participants watched staged theft footage (thief white or Black). White participants were more accurate identifying the white thief — and their confidence was higher for the white thief. Confidence and accuracy move together only for own-race faces.
Brigham et al. (1982)Field study: convenience store cashiers (64 white, 9 Black) identified a customer from a photo line-up. Only a small own-race effect in accuracy — but white cashiers' recognition of the Black customer related to self-reported cross-racial experiences. Supports the theory that experience influences recognition.
Platz and Hosch (1988)Replication of Brigham's field study: the other-race effect extended to identification of Hispanic customers too.
Hourihan et al. (2012)102 participants (white n=50, Asian n=52): white participants were superior at recognising white and East Asian faces; East Asian participants were better at recognising different faces. Possibly due to media exposure — American shows aired in China, few Chinese shows in America. Pre-ratings of likely recognition predicted accuracy.
Hancock and Rhodes (2008)Higher contact with other-race groups (living in an other-race country) was associated with increased other-race facial recognition — exposure reduces the effect.
Sangrigoli et al. (2005)Adult Koreans adopted by European white families as children were better at identifying white faces; non-adopted Koreans were better at identifying East Asian faces. Early childhood exposure to other-race faces can reduce own-race bias.
Meissner and Brigham (2001)Meta-analysis of 39 cross-race identification articles across three decades: own-race faces were 1.40 times more likely to be correctly identified and 1.56 times less likely to be falsely identified.

Evaluation 评价

It converges from many directions

Archival data (Scheck), field studies (Brigham, Platz and Hosch), lab recognition tests (Hourihan, Smith), natural experiments (Sangrigoli) and a meta-analysis (Meissner and Brigham) all point to the same effect.

Evidence archival + field + lab + natural experiment = unusually strong support.

The experience finding is useful

Brigham (cross-racial experience), Hancock and Rhodes (contact) and Sangrigoli (adoption) all suggest experience with other-race faces improves recognition.

Application the effect is not fixed — exposure and training can reduce it.

Lack of ecological validity

Much of the research is conducted in laboratory settings using isolated photographs of faces. It does not involve real witnesses to real crimes, so it may tell us little about how the other-race effect influences real eyewitness testimony.

Ecological validity Why it matters: recognising a photo and identifying a criminal are not the same task.

Confidence is not accuracy

Smith (2004) found white participants felt more confident about identifying the white thief. Juries are swayed by confidence — but this chapter has shown confidence and accuracy can come apart.

Misapplied in court Why it matters: a confident witness of an other-race perpetrator may be precisely the least reliable one.

17. Exam Lab

🧭 IDEA — how to answer a question with a scenario

  • I — Identify: which factor is the scenario showing? (Check the timeline: while watching, or afterwards?)
  • E — Explain: state the factor and its mechanism in its own terms.
  • A — Apply: tie it to the details — name the object, the person, the timing.

A sentence that says what earns one mark; the same sentence plus why it matters earns two.

✍️ Practice 1 — 2 marks

Describe how stress may influence the reliability of eyewitness memory.

Model answer

  • Stress increases arousal; according to the Yerkes–Dodson Law, once arousal passes the optimum level, performance — including recall of an event — declines.
  • Stress also narrows attention (Easterbrook, 1959): the witness encodes only central details and misses peripheral information, so their testimony is incomplete even if it feels vivid.

✍️ Practice 2 — 1 mark

State what is meant by the term 'weapon focus' as a factor that influences eyewitness memory.

Weapon focus is an eyewitness's concentration on a weapon to the exclusion of other details of a crime.

✍️ Practice 3 — 1 mark

Define 'post-event information' as a factor that influences eyewitness memory.

Post-event information is any information after the witnessed event which may influence the original memory of the incident.

✍️ Practice 4 — 2 marks

Explain one reason why weapon focus may influence eyewitness memory.

Model answer

The presence of a weapon narrowes the witness's attention onto the weapon itself, so other important details — especially the perpetrator's face — are never encoded into memory. Because those details were never stored, the witness cannot later identify the offender: Loftus et al. (1987) found only 11% of weapon-viewers identified the customer, versus 39% with a chequebook.

✍️ Practice 5 — 2 marks

Explain one ethical issue that should be considered when researching factors that influence eyewitness memory.

Model answer

Research may involve exposing participants to a traumatic event or distressing film footage, which risks psychological harm. Using real witnesses raises the same issue differently: questioning forces them to relive the incident, which may cause distress — so researchers must weigh the value of the findings against protection from harm.

✍️ Practice 6 — 8 marks

Evaluate the reliability of eyewitness testimony.

Half the marks come from accurate content, half from judgement with evidence. Plan four paragraphs:

ParagraphWhat goes in it
1Why EWT matters: the most common form of evidence, trusted by juries — yet incorrect eyewitness accounts are the leading cause of miscarriages of justice.
2Evidence that it is unreliable: lab studies of post-event information (Gabbert 71%), stress (Morgan 1 in 3; Deffenbacher meta-analysis), weapon focus (Loftus 11% vs 39%; Fawcett meta-analysis), other-race effect (Meissner and Brigham 1.40×).
3Evidence it can be reliable: real-life witnesses were not misled (Yuille and Cutshall), stayed accurate after trauma (Thompson 74/86; Rinolo 15/20; Odinot 84%), the effect can be overcome (Pickel 2006), and lab findings may overstate fragility because they lack real stress (ecological validity).
4A conclusion that answers the question: EWT is sometimes reliable and sometimes not — reliability depends on the factor involved, the stressfulness of the event and the witness's experience. Courts should treat confidence as separate from accuracy.

Model answer — four developed points

  • Unreliable — post-event information. Memory is reconstructive, so information after the event can alter it. Gabbert, Memon and Allen (2003) found 71% of witnesses who discussed the event recalled items not in their own video. This suggests testimony can be contaminated long before it reaches court.
  • Unreliable — stress and weapon focus. High arousal narrows attention onto central details: Morgan III et al. (2007) found 1 in 3 personnel could not identify their interrogator after acute stress, and Loftus et al. (1987) found weapon viewers identified the customer only 11% of the time. Deffenbacher et al.'s (2004) meta-analysis confirms stress overall impairs recall.
  • Reliable — real-life evidence. Witnesses of real events resist the effects predicted by the lab: Yuille and Cutshall's (1986) witnesses were not misled by leading questions; Thompson et al. (1997) confirmed 74 of 86 statements from the Marchioness sinking; Odinot (2008) found 84% of recall accurate. This suggests laboratory studies — lacking genuine stress — may overstate unreliability.
  • Conclusion. EWT is not unreliable in general; it is conditionally reliable. Stress, weapons and post-event information measurably degrade it in controlled research, but real witnesses with close, meaningful involvement often remember well. The practical implication is that courts should weigh the conditions of the witness — and remember that confidence is not the same as accuracy.

Why this scores: each point names evidence with numbers, and the conclusion answers "how reliable" rather than repeating the description.

🔑 Exam key points — what a strong answer contains ›
  1. Eyewitness testimony is the most common evidence in court — and incorrect eyewitness accounts are the leading cause of miscarriages of justice.
  2. Post-event information = information after the event that alters the original memory: discussions (Gabbert: 71%, memory conformity), leading questions (Poole and Lindsay: 95% of children), media.
  3. Real-life witnesses resist: Yuille and Cutshall (not misled), Odinot (84% accurate) — lab vs real life is the central evaluation.
  4. Stress: Yerkes–Dodson (optimum arousal), Easterbrook (attentional narrowing), source monitoring (Crombag: 60% "saw" footage that never existed).
  5. Weapon focus has two explanations — stress and attention — and Pickel's salon study shows unusualness beats threat (raw chicken!).
  6. Other-race effect: same-race faces recognised more accurately (Meissner and Brigham: 1.40×); explained by encoding differences (Valentine 1991) and in-group/out-group processing; experience reduces it (Sangrigoli).
  7. Every criticism needs a why it matters sentence — and the recurring ones here are ecological validity, ethics and the meaning of "arousal".
  8. In scenario answers, first ask: did the problem happen while watching or afterwards?

18. Key Terms

eyewitness目击证人
An individual who sees an event such as a crime occurring.
eyewitness testimony目击证人证词
The account an eyewitness gives of what they saw, to the police and in court.
post-event information事件后信息
Any information after the witnessed event which may influence the original memory of the incident.
leading question引导性问题
A question that suggests the answer it wants, e.g. "How tall was the person?".
reconstructive memory重构记忆
We rebuild memories using main points and interpretation, influenced by schemas and cultural expectations.
schema图式
A mental framework of expectations about what usually happens in an event.
misinformation effect错误信息效应
Memory becomes less accurate because of exposure to post-event information (Loftus).
memory conformity记忆从众
Witness memories become similar to one another because of discussion (Gabbert, Memon and Allen, 2003).
arousal唤醒
The level of physiological and psychological alertness, from sleepy to highly stressed.
Yerkes–Dodson Law耶克斯–多德森定律
Performance rises with arousal to an optimum point, then declines as arousal gets too high (1908).
attentional narrowing注意狭窄
Under high emotional arousal, attention narrows and peripheral details are not encoded (Easterbrook, 1959).
source monitoring来源监控
Distinguishing where information came from; trauma survivors may confuse post-event information with what actually happened.
weapon focus武器聚焦
An eyewitness's concentration on a weapon to the exclusion of other details of a crime.
other-race effect异族效应
Same-race faces are recognised more accurately than faces of a different race; also called the cross-race effect.
in-group内群体
A group to which we have membership; in-group faces are processed as individuals.
out-group外群体
A group to which we do not have membership; out-group faces are only categorised, not individuated.
ecological validity生态效度
Whether findings from artificial settings apply to real-life situations.
miscarriage of justice司法误判
A wrongful conviction; incorrect eyewitness accounts are its leading cause.
meta-analysis元分析
A statistical combination of many studies, e.g. Deffenbacher et al. (2004); Meissner and Brigham (2001).
cognitive interview认知访谈
Police technique using open, non-leading questions, designed to reduce post-event contamination of memory.
