The whole chapter in one sentence 全章一句话
When a unanimous majority deliberately gave an obviously wrong answer, 32% of responses followed — but break the unanimity with one ally (5.5–13%), shrink or grow the majority past three, or let the participant answer in private (7.4–12.5%), and conformity collapses: people yield to normative pressure, not to information.

§1 · The task, and why the obvious answer is the pointANSWERS → p.19
The setup

Solomon Asch conducted a series of experiments designed to test influence on conformity — the factors which affect conformity levels. Typically, naive participant was placed in a group of to see whether they would conform to the group norm.type of experiment / majority / who else is in the group

The task

Match the length of a line with one of comparison lines of unequal length — one of which matched the standard line. The correct answer is .what you look at / how many / how hard is it

中文理解：为什么“答案明显”是整个设计的核心
1.1.4 里 Sherif 用的是模糊任务（没人知道光点动了多远）—— 从众可以说是“为了获得正确信息”（ISI）。
Asch 反着来：答案一眼就能看出对错。这时候再从众，唯一的解释就是规范性社会影响 NSI —— 屈服于压力，而不是寻找信息。
两个实验放在一起记：Sherif = 模糊任务 → ISI；Asch = 明显任务 → NSI。

§8 · Asch (1956) — the classic versionANSWERS → p.19
Procedure

college students in groups of to nine. The naive participant sat in the chair. Across critical trials the majority's wrong answer was either or different from the standard line. Public answers were compared against a group who wrote their judgements privately.

Results
  • The control group of private responders gave only incorrect judgements in total — versus about of responses wrong in the experimental group.
  • When the majority chose a different (incorrect) line, naive participants also made a selection.
  • With an judgement, modelled the judgement and made a — selecting the moderately different line instead.
  • Again: some maintained absolute , some yielded .
中文理解：“妥协”这个细节最值钱
多数人指着一个极端错的答案，五分之一的人不是照抄、而是选了个折中的错误 —— 压力之下人会真的扭曲自己的判断，不是简单复制别人的答案。写 evaluation 或 application 时这是很鲜活的细节。

§9 · Asch (1956) continued — discs and privacyANSWERS → p.19
Beyond lines: the coloured discs

To test whether conformity extended to other perceived stimuli, participants judged whether a comparison was brighter or darker than a standard. Over 12 trials where the group deliberately judged wrongly, % of the naive participants' responses were incorrect.

The privacy variation

When the group judged but the naive participant answered , conformity dropped to just % incorrect estimates.

The conclusions of the 1956 series
  • Participants were complying because of social influence.
  • factors — social support and the size of the majority — influence conformity rates.
  • differences between people also affect how they respond.

Answer Key · Part 2 — exam model answers 答案速查（二）Mark honestly, then re-write in full
p.17 — Q1 model (2 marks)

① The procedure was standardised — every participant faced the same line judgements in the same way, so conformity was measured objectively through the error rate. ② The use of a unanimous confederate majority against a single naive participant created a controlled, unambiguous test of majority pressure.

p.17 — Q2 model (4 marks)

Asch placed 50 male college students in groups of eight and asked them to publicly judge which of three comparison lines matched a standard line. Unknown to one naive participant, the other seven members were confederates who deliberately gave the same wrong answer, so the participant faced a unanimous incorrect majority across 12 critical trials. The frequency of incorrect responses was the measure of conformity: 32% of estimates followed the majority's error and 68% were correct. Participants were interviewed and debriefed immediately afterwards.

p.17 — Q3 model (2 marks)

The control group wrote their answers in private and erred on only 7.4% of responses (1952; in 1956, 37 private responders made just 3 errors), compared with about a third of public answers following the majority. This shows the public errors were caused by social pressure rather than the difficulty of the task — privately, participants almost never misjudged the lines.

p.17 — Q4 model (2 marks)

When one confederate consistently gave the correct answer, conformity fell from around 32% to 13% (1952; 5.5% in the 1951 ally variation). Because the only change was the presence of one ally, the drop shows that a partner acts as social support against majority pressure — breaking the group's unanimity removes most of its power.

p.17 — Q5 model (4 marks)

Improvement: use a more representative sample that includes female participants instead of just the 50 male students. Justification: this would make his findings about conformity to a majority group more generalisable to a wider target population, so we could judge whether the 32% rate applies to women as well.
Alternative: a more realistic task, such as photographs of real objects, which would increase the validity of Asch's findings about conformity in real-life situations.

p.18 — Q6 model (8 marks)

¶1. A strength is that the procedure was highly standardised and controlled: every participant faced the same line judgements in exactly the same way, and conformity was measured objectively through the error rate. This means the study is replicable, so reliability can be checked, and the control increases internal validity — the error rates were affected only by the majority's answers, and no other variable.

¶2. However, the findings may not extend beyond the laboratory. Conformity to a trivial line judgement may occur under laboratory pressure, but real-life conformity involves decisions of greater importance, where the pressure may not exist or may be even stronger. This limits the ecological validity, so 32% cannot be taken as a measure of everyday conformity.

¶3. The research also raises ethical problems: participants were deceived about the nature of the study and the identity of the group members, and experienced stress when giving public answers against a unanimous majority. Asch defended this because the deception was a necessary part of the procedure — participants who knew the truth would have acted very differently — but informed consent was still impossible.

¶4. Finally, the sample — American male university students in the 1950s — limits generalisation, and later research with different populations has not produced the same conformity rates, suggesting conformity is not a stable human characteristic. Mori and Arai (2010) used familiar groups and found males, but not females, less likely to be swayed, showing the effect depends on who the group is. On balance, Asch's research remains a convincing demonstration of normative influence, but its figures are specific to its time, task and sample.
